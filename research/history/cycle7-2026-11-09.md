## Scope retained

The review asks how interactions with generative AI affect the **development, exercise, and justification of judgement** in complex tasks among adult learners and professionals. Analytical moves include framing, comparing alternatives, critiquing, revising, negotiating feedback, and working across individual, group, teacher and AI contributions. Quality of assisted output is distinct from perceived learning, observed judgement processes, and evidence of independent capability or transfer.

User-directed scope decisions stand: D3 and D4 were deprioritised; standalone AI credibility, hallucination detection and model accuracy research are not a principal strand. Checking is relevant only when situated inside evaluation, reflection or responsibility for decisions.

## How this screen was conducted

- **Input basis:** titles, abstracts and metadata from the user's unmodified Scopus A, B and C CSV exports; deduplicated source master is retained.
- **Population:** adult learners/practitioners preferred; analogous undergraduate and preservice education tasks sometimes retained, with a transferability caveat.
- **Technology:** generative AI interaction central to Core status; mixed digital technologies or human-only feedback can be Contextual where the mechanism clearly informs the review.
- **Procedure:** deterministic lexical triage informed by v0.2, followed by targeted second readings of abstracts and specific overrides. Existing pilot decisions were not silently overwritten by automated scoring. Fourteen human-adjudicated decisions were frozen.
- **Consistency pass:** identified and corrected some false exclusions and overconfident Core labels. Thirty initially excluded records were randomly sampled (fixed seed 20261108): ten were reclassified as contextual/core after closer reading (nine contextual, one core). A separate sweep of 21 high-risk exclusions and an expanded recheck rescued sixteen additional exclusions. The resulting 98-record QC/uncertain queue is a prioritised review set; their presence in that queue is **not** equivalent to independent adjudication.
- **Limits:** a single AI-assisted screener is not independent human dual screening. Confidence fields indicate abstract-level ambiguity, not peer-reviewed evidence strength. Results and counts should be called *provisional* for any eventual PRISMA-style diagram until researcher verification.
- **Screening model versions:** pilot v0.1, researcher v0.2 calibration, full v0.2 pass. Do not invent an inter-rater reliability statistic or call the review systematic.

## A. Research scope and question (provisional)

**Primary review question:** How does interaction with generative AI shape the development, exercise and justification of judgement among adult learners and practitioners undertaking complex professional tasks?

**Situated question for the UCL study:** How do postgraduate policy students develop, exercise and explain judgement when working individually, with peers, with educators and with PolicyMemo.ai? Which observed differences can reasonably be interpreted as learning, as situated AI-assisted performance, or as redistributed judgement?

The second question does not assume that every postgraduate participant has substantial professional experience. Prior work experience is an empirical characteristic to establish, not an inclusion assumption.

**Design decision:** Keep this as a **focused, critical narrative review with a transparent pilot search and selection trail**. It is not a meta-analysis, systematic review or a claim of exhaustive coverage. The goal is explanatory synthesis that can inform study design and later publication, rather than aggregating incompatible proxies for critical thinking.

### A1. Search strands

1. **Professional learning, reflective practice and critique.** Reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action; feedback literacy and evaluative judgement; design studio critique; professional expertise, practice and transfer. Foundational texts can predate 2020 if conceptually necessary; they must be read in original form before quotation or detailed attribution.
2. **AI-mediated scaffolding, feedback and problem framing.** Prompting, coaching, challenge, alternate framings and feedback; design of tools that elicit reasoning rather than supply answers.
3. **Judgement, agency and delegation.** Accept/reject/adapt decisions, justified reliance, task allocation, cognitive offloading and the ownership of decisions. Evaluation of AI factual correctness is not itself a review strand.
4. **Individual–peer–teacher–AI interaction.** Reasoning as social practice, feedback and critique; task sequencing; peer discussion and distributed judgement; AI as a distinct participant in a learning ecology rather than an isolated treatment.
5. **Performance versus learning and transfer.** Quality of assisted output; reasoning process; later unaided performance; supported performance on novel tasks; retention, confidence and self-perception. These are *different* constructs and should not be collapsed.

### A2. Provisional pilot eligibility

- **Population:** Core: adult postgraduate learners, adult professionals or public-sector/policy practitioners doing complex analytical work. Adjacent: other university students whose tasks plausibly resemble policy reasoning; include only with explicit transferability caveats. K–12 evidence can illuminate mechanisms and tool design but is **not core population evidence**.
- **Intervention/exposure:** Conversational or generative AI, including domain-specific AI agents, chat interfaces and human–AI collaborative workflows. Non-generative automation can be used as theoretical context, not silently pooled with generative AI findings.
- **Relevant phenomena:** Problem framing, evidence evaluation, reasoning/argumentation, justified choices, feedback and critique, intellectual agency, reliance/offloading, or learning versus performance. Pure speed/productivity results are context unless linked to judgement or learning.
- **Methods:** Empirical original studies and substantive systematic/scoping reviews or meta-analyses; include qualitative and mixed-methods research. Foundational theory and design/position papers remain in a labelled *conceptual/design* stream, not in the empirical-effects stream.
- **Period:** Initial empirical search window 7 November 2020–7 November 2026, with emphasis on the post-November-2022 generative-AI period. Do not exclude foundational work on date alone.
- **Methodological relevance:** Record actual sample, intervention, comparator, independent versus assisted assessments, observation versus self-report, baseline equivalence, follow-up, conflicts of interest and construct validity. Negative/null findings are explicitly eligible.
- **Screening levels:** Core / adjacent / conceptual or context / deferred or exclude. Inclusion for narrative discussion is **not** endorsement of a study's causal claims.

**Trade-off:** A tight postgraduate-only criterion would miss comparative experiments on how AI scaffolding works; admitting every student-facing AI study would swamp professional judgement with language-learning and short task outcomes. Use tiered relevance plus methodological appraisal. In the eventual written review, separate evidence of effects on *scores* from evidence of mechanisms of *judgement*.

## C. First-batch critical appraisal: three starting papers

### C1. Deng et al. (2025), *Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies*

**Citation:** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y. & Liu, S. (2025). *Computers & Education*, 227, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224. Full text: local uploaded `1-s2.0-S0360131524002380-main.pdf`.

**Question and scope.** What does experimental and quasi-experimental evidence say about ChatGPT's effects on different dimensions of student learning, and how do settings and methods shape reported effects? Search through **31 August 2024** in EBSCOhost, IEEE Xplore, PsycINFO, Scopus and Web of Science. PRISMA screening: 1,683 database records, 69 included articles; 62 contributed to quantitative meta-analyses. This is **the authors' database search**, not ours (PDF pp. 6–10, particularly p. 6 search description and p. 9 analysis).

**Population and interventions.** Educational studies, predominantly university students, with a range of subjects but an emphasis on language education and classroom use. Interventions involved ChatGPT, not all generative-AI systems or all agentic workflows. Designs include experimental/quasi-experimental group comparisons. The included participants and contexts are heterogeneous, not a focused sample of adult policy practitioners (pp. 10–12).

**Methods and appraisal.** The review used quantitative study-quality criteria (Kmet et al.), dual independent rating and inter-rater agreement; random-effects meta-analysis of Hedges' g. Positive feature: explicit search, eligibility, review process and sensitivity/bias analyses. Limitation: outcome families are broad, varied across fields; the review deliberately excluded qualitative and within-subject studies, which often illuminate *how* people reason. Heterogeneity across outcomes is exceptionally high; publication-bias tests flag potential issues for academic performance and affective/motivational outcomes (pp. 6–7, 9, 12–13).

**Findings (authors' pooled estimates, PDF p. 13, Table 7):** academic performance **g = 0.712** (95% CI 0.497–0.926; **I² = 91.8%**); affective-motivational states **g = 0.881** (I² = 92.9%); higher-order-thinking **propensities** **g = 0.703** (I² = 90.3%); self-efficacy **g = 0.441**, CI crossing zero; mental effort **g = −0.675**, based on only four effect sizes. These are not uniform effects on one stable construct.

**Crucial limitations.** For academic-performance studies, nine explicitly **allowed** ChatGPT during post-intervention assessment, while 33 did **not state whether it was allowed** (p. 18). This means assisted product quality can be mistaken for learning. The higher-order-thinking outcome was often *self-reported propensity/disposition*, not demonstrated performance (p. 20). Just **five of 62** meta-analysed studies reported power analysis; baseline differences were handled unevenly (pp. 15–16 and 21–22). Motivational benefits might reflect novelty; the authors note limited repeated measurement during the intervention (p. 19).

**Assessment of evidential weight.** **Moderate for the broad claim** that many studied interventions improve measured educational outcomes; **low for a claim about durable, independent policy judgement**. Very high heterogeneity should block translating a pooled g into an expected effect in PolicyMemo.ai.

**Implication for PolicyMemo.ai.** Keep *assisted artefact quality*, *justification/process*, *quality of policy judgement* and *learning over time* distinct. Prompt/AI traces alone cannot establish either improvement or deterioration. Do not equate lower mental effort with lower learning: offloading could remove low-value work or displace necessary practice.

**Citation leads:** Niloy et al. (2024) (negative originality outcome); original studies measuring unaided outcomes, multiweek interventions or follow-up; scrutinise samples, assessment conditions and whether critical thinking is self-reported.

### C2. Cong-Lem & Nguyen (2026), *Towards a framework for understanding and assessing critical thinking in generative AI-enhanced learning environments: a scoping review*

**Citation:** Cong-Lem, N. & Nguyen, T. T.-D. (2026). *European Journal of Psychology of Education*, 41, article 41. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10212-026-01089-y. Full text: uploaded `s10212-026-01089-y.pdf` (25 pages, article page numbering 1–25).

**Question and scope.** How has critical thinking (CT) been conceptualised and measured in GenAI-enhanced learning, and what impacts are reported? Structured review of empirical educational studies from November 2022 to January 2025; searches in Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar on 6 February 2025 yielded 1,991 records; **29** were included (pp. 3–7). This was **the authors' formal search**, not ours.

**Population and methods.** Primarily higher education: **27/29** university studies, but mixed undergraduate/postgraduate populations. Quantitative (17), mixed methods (11) and qualitative (1). Two-reviewer title/abstract and full-text screening, Covidence workflow; Google Scholar screened only first 100 records. The Scopus query placed `AI/ChatGPT` and `critical thinking` in title fields and assessment-related terms in title/abstract/keywords, potentially missing relevant studies using judgement/decision language rather than *critical thinking* (pp. 4–9).

**Findings.** Three important contributions: (1) CT means different things in different papers, from reflective judgement to verification and detecting GenAI errors; (2) measurement ranges from self-report scales to contextual tasks; (3) authors classify **21/29** studies as positive, seven as mixed and one as negative (pp. 9–13). Their four-part proposed model is **analytical reasoning, source verification, metacognitive self-regulation and CT dispositions** (pp. 13–15).

**Crucial limitations.** A **scoping review without formal study-level quality/risk-of-bias appraisal** (p. 16). Counting 21 positive studies does not mean there is a 72.4% probability that AI improves CT, nor that the positive studies are the strongest. The title-dependent search and restriction to peer-reviewed journals may underrepresent professional judgement, negative/null results, conference HCI research and post-January-2025 studies. Its proposed model is conceptually useful but not thereby a validated predictive theory (pp. 4–6, 16).

**Assessment of evidential weight.** **High usefulness for construct mapping; limited evidential weight for a causal effect estimate.** Avoid writing that the four dimensions have been empirically proven to cause learning; that overreads a synthesis proposing a framework.

**Implication for PolicyMemo.ai.** Treat verification, justification, metacognitive regulation and ownership of judgement as *possible observables*, not a compulsory four-item checklist. In particular, students can rationally accept accurate AI contributions; identifying an error is not the sole sign of critical thinking. Add attention to policy-specific normative judgement, contestable values and institutional/political feasibility—elements not guaranteed by generic CT scales.

**Citation leads:** Essien et al. (2024; UK postgraduates); de la Puente et al. (2024; international-relations debates); guidance-based instructional designs and explicit individual/peer/AI sequences (specific originals to verify before citing as evidence). The review explicitly excludes workplace/professional studies outside educational settings (p. 6): a **known search blind spot** for our review.

### C3. Casey (2026), *ChatGPT in public policy teaching and assessment: An examination of opportunities and challenges*

**Citation:** Casey, D. (2026 issue; accepted 2024). *Australian Journal of Public Administration*, 85, 362–376. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8500.12647. Full text: uploaded `Aust J Public Adm - 2024 - Casey - ... .pdf` (15 pages; publisher pages 362–376).

**Question and setting.** How do policy students understand ChatGPT in teaching/assessment, and its potential use in policy formulation? A **Bachelor of Public Policy capstone** at the Australian National University, not postgraduate policy training. All 24 enrolled students used ChatGPT to draft a decision brief and then reflected; **seven of 24 consented** to research use of their essays and transcripts, and **five** attended a later focus group. Qualitative inductive thematic coding; ChatGPT was also used to assist coding/themes (pp. 366–368 and appendix p. 376).

**Findings.** Participants reported recognising weak nuance, problems with source transparency, insufficient consideration of politics, governance and fairness; some saw the exercise as useful for AI literacy and critique/evaluative judgement. The paper suggests tasks requiring deliberate analysis of AI output, not just its production, have educational promise. Student scepticism regarding policy use was salient (pp. 367–370).

**Crucial limitations.** Tiny volunteer sample in one university and a **deliberately artificial task** requiring ChatGPT to produce the brief, using the 2023 free ChatGPT 3.5 generation; students' reports cannot demonstrate durable improvements in reasoning. Participation is self-selected; perceptions may be shaped by briefing, grading and the intentionally constrained exercise. Researcher-teacher dual role and ChatGPT-assisted qualitative coding warrant a transparent interpretive audit (pp. 366–367, 370–371).

**Assessment of evidential weight.** **High contextual relevance to policy reasoning; low causal weight for learning gains.** This is an instructive qualitative case, not a demonstration that critique training enhances independent judgement.

**Implication for PolicyMemo.ai.** Use policy-domain-specific coding of *why* an output was adapted or rejected: problem diagnosis, strength of evidence, competing values, institutional feasibility, uncertainty and authoriser context. Give space for students to **defend a warranted acceptance** of a tool suggestion, not just report its mistakes. Consider eliciting their judgement before and after AI, with reasoning notes and peer discussion context; any new data collection must be ethics-approved.

### D1. Lee et al. (2025): professional critical thinking as stewardship — **core; full author PDF**

**Reference:** Lee, H.-P. et al. (2025), *The impact of generative AI on critical thinking: Self-reported reductions in cognitive effort and confidence effects from a survey of knowledge workers*, *CHI '25*, article 1121. https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778; author PDF: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf.

**Question/method/population:** Survey of **319** knowledge workers recruited through Prolific who reported using GenAI at work weekly; **936** detailed, recalled workplace task examples. Quantitative models of **self-reported** enactment and effort associated with CT; qualitative coding of examples. Participants had varied jobs, so not all have policy expertise or verified professional seniority (full PDF §§3–6).

**Findings:** Greater task-specific confidence in AI is associated with reduced perceived CT effort/enactment; greater self-confidence is associated with more. Workers describe shifts **from information gathering to verification**, **from execution to response integration**, and **from task execution to stewardship**. Their own discussion warns that reduced effort may represent useful support, offloading, less CT, or a confusion between mental effort and CT effort (§§4–6).

**Appraisal:** Particularly good **conceptual and ecological proximity** to professional work, and unusually sensitive to altered *forms* of judgement. But a single cross-sectional retrospective survey cannot causally measure cognitive decline, skill transfer or actual effort; users self-select into examples and assess their own CT (limitations §6.3). **Usefulness: high for mechanisms and interview vocabulary, low for causal learning effects.**

### D2. Essien et al. (2024): postgraduate education, but compromised comparison — **core; full publisher PDF text**

**Reference:** Essien, A. E., Bukoye, O. T., O'Dea, X. & Kremantzis, M. D. (2024), *The influence of AI text generators on critical thinking skills in UK business schools*, *Studies in Higher Education* 49(5), 865–882. https://doi.org/10.1080/03075079.2024.2316881. Full publisher PDF: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/03075079.2024.2316881.

**Question/method/population:** A reported **107 UK postgraduate business students** in a mixed-method/quasi-experimental design framed around Bloom's taxonomy; researchers discuss control and ChatGPT intervention groups. **Crucial detail:** the methods explicitly state that the *post-test was conducted exclusively with the experimental group* (publisher pp. 869–870, §3.1). Authors report bigger changes at lower Bloom levels than higher ones, and raise reliability and accuracy concerns.

**Appraisal:** Population is highly comparable. However, the reported one-group follow-up undermines the ability to estimate a treatment-versus-control difference after exposure, even if groups were divided at baseline; paired pre/post improvement may reflect maturation, repeated assessment or other exposures. There are also complexities in matching reported sample and statistical tables that need detailed verification. **Do not cite this as strong causal evidence of AI improving postgraduate CT.** Use as a useful *case of outcome-measure and design ambiguity*, with high setting relevance but low causal certainty.

### D5. Wiles et al. (2026): assisted capability versus independently assessed knowledge — **core empirical; full article appraised Cycle 2**

**Reference and access:** Wiles, E., Krayer, L., Abbadi, M., Awasthi, U., Kennedy, R., Mishkin, P., Sack, D. & Candelon, F. (2026), *Generative AI and the temporary upskilling of knowledge workers*, *Nature Human Behaviour*, published online 26 October 2026. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-026-02566-6. The 15-page uploaded publisher PDF `s41562-026-02566-6.pdf` contains the article on PDF pp. 1–8 and a reporting summary on pp. 9–15. The PDF cover carries a placeholder publication date; the publisher and PubMed confirm **26 October 2026**. All page references below refer to the uploaded PDF unless a journal page is given.

**Research question and construct.** Can GenAI enable non-technical knowledge workers to perform tasks outside their existing skills, and does this translate into independently assessed knowledge? The paper deliberately separates *new capability with a technological aid* from *acquisition of knowledge without that aid* (p. 1). These are not interchangeable definitions of professional learning, but they clarify why an improved deliverable is an inadequate sole measure of skill acquisition.

**Design, participants and comparator.** Preregistered, stratified randomised experiment conducted at Boston Consulting Group in March–April 2024. A total of **986** consultants were allocated equally; **573** began the survey, **487** submitted both assigned tasks and formed the principal analysis sample. Participants were educated adult knowledge workers, though not presumed to be expert data scientists. Treatment included 15–20 minutes of ChatGPT prompting training **plus access to GPT-4**; controls received comparable-duration training in web search and public technical resources (Google, Stack Overflow, Khan Academy). Each did two randomly assigned 90-minute tasks drawn from coding/data cleaning, statistical interpretation, and prediction/modelling. Tasks were designed jointly by BCG data scientists and an OpenAI research team so ChatGPT could help but could not reliably complete them unassisted (pp. 1–2, 5–6; Table 1 p. 2).

**Performance results.** Compared with controls, treated workers scored **49 percentage points higher on coding** (95% CI 42–56), **20 points on statistics** (95% CI 15–25), and **17 points on prediction** (95% CI 9–25); task scales were anchored to the average performance of 44 BCG data scientists, not to an educational learning standard (pp. 2–3, Fig. 1). Their performance was generally below the expert benchmark, though a subgroup with prior coding experience reached statistically indistinguishable performance on coding/statistics (pp. 3–4, Fig. 2); the latter is not evidence of full professional equivalence.

**Independent assessment and null result.** After the work session, all participants answered a short set of five technical questions without ChatGPT. The treatment group did **not** demonstrate a statistically detectable gain over control (joint test *F*(5,198)=0.83, *p*=.531; individual question Bayes factors 0.10, 0.18, 0.50, 0.13, 0.62; p. 4, Fig. 3). The paper tests **immediate unaided technical recall/application on five items**, not the durability of learning over weeks or development of reflective judgement. ‘No evidence of a difference’ is not equivalent to proving that no learning occurred.

**Additional outcomes outside our active scope.** Authors also explored participants' forecasting of AI capability boundaries (pp. 4–5). That outcome was **not preregistered**; it is not central to this review and should not displace the performance-versus-learning distinction.

**Internal validity/limitations.** Random allocation, preregistration and transparent reporting strengthen causal inference for the specific treatment package. However: (i) **487 of 986** allocated participants formed the final principal sample, creating selection/attrition concerns; authors report balance checks and Lee bounds for their primary effects (pp. 2, 6), but these cannot rule out all unobserved differences; (ii) intervention confounds AI access with training in its use; (iii) a single short, time-pressured session is not a period of sustained workplace learning; (iv) the post-test is a very narrow knowledge proxy and an immediate assessment; (v) complex tasks were discrete data-science components rather than end-to-end work; (vi) parts of free-text assessment were scored by GPT-4 Turbo API with data scientist validation; full scoring and supplementary robustness materials were **not separately appraised** in this cycle (p. 7); (vii) research design and interpretation involved employees of BCG/OpenAI, with those relationships disclosed (p. 8).

**Relevance to PolicyMemo.ai.** **High for the distinction between assisted performance and demonstrated acquisition; moderate for judgement-process mechanisms; indirect for policy reasoning.** A student may produce a stronger policy analysis without becoming more capable of generating the same analysis unaided. Equally, some capability may properly be *relational*, exercised through tools and colleagues. Wiles does not directly observe how participants frame policy problems, negotiate critique, or justify trade-offs. Do not describe it as evidence that AI is educationally harmful, or that unaided testing is the gold standard for all valuable learning.

**Analytical decision:** include Wiles as a central **boundary condition** for assessing learning claims, but place it next to the theory of *evaluative judgement* (Tai et al.) and *reflective practice/critique* (Schön and studio studies), not in a generic harms-and-trust section.

## E. Emerging synthesis — hypotheses to test, not established conclusions

**E1. AI can redistribute effort, not simply add/subtract thinking.** Lee documents changes in the *location* of judgement (verification, integration, stewardship), while Deng aggregates broad improvements in conventional outcome scores. Neither independently proves a net gain or loss in learning. Some offloading may be an appropriate division of labour; whether it is educationally harmful depends on which cognitive activity is outsourced and why.

**E2. What is being learned to judge?** Tai and the studio-critique tradition draw attention to the capability to recognise and justify *quality* in one's own and others' work. For policy analysis that includes diagnosis, choice of evaluative criteria, values, trade-offs, feasibility and fit to the authorising context. A change in the quality of a memo is not necessarily a change in this evaluative capability; students' reasons, revisions, and interactions must be studied.

**E3. Critique is socially situated.** Casey uses an AI brief as a common object for critique, but did not isolate the roles of peers, teacher and AI. Studio and seminar dialogue can bundle student, peer, tutor, and tool contributions. Our study should observe which party introduced a reframing or challenge and how it was negotiated, not automatically attribute a revised memo to AI.

**E4. Assistance and skill acquisition can diverge.** Deng's assessments often do not separate tool use from learner ability. Wiles (full article) tests a sharper boundary in adults and finds no measurable gain on a short, immediate no-AI technical quiz. But *an unaided task alone is not exhaustive evidence of learning*: learning may include stronger supported judgement, better questions, improved collaboration or appropriate delegation. More than one outcome is needed.

**E5. Study design needs a mechanism, not a predetermined victory story.** A credible qualitative study should be able to identify productive AI scaffolding, justified refusal, justified acceptance, premature closure, superficial copying, productive offloading, peer-mediated correction and no change. 'Detects an AI error' is a possible indicator—not the master definition of CT. 'Produced a better memo' is a possible outcome—not proof of learning.

### Explicit uncertainties and gaps

- What does **policy-specific judgement** comprise beyond generic critical-thinking scales: framing contested problems, evaluating evidence/uncertainty, recognising trade-offs, handling legitimacy and feasibility, and defending proposals to authorisers?
- Few mapped educational reviews include **actual workplace/professional** populations or recent **agent-based workflows**. A focused professional-study strand is necessary.
- Comparisons of **AI-first versus human/peer-first sequencing** need direct empirical studies. The current first batch does not demonstrate which ordering is best.
- **Experienced versus less experienced adult learners** might use AI differently; do not presume experience from postgraduate enrolment.
- Self-report, observable trace, assisted quality and independent application require distinct measurement strategies and analytic claims.
- We have not yet closely read Schön's books. We have read Tai and the full text of Oh et al. and located original design-studio studies, including Goldschmidt et al. (2010) and Cennamo et al. (2011); the latter need deeper method appraisal. Jon Kolko's essays are routes to originals, not original empirical evidence for this review. Mollick et al.'s 2024 *Simulated Practice at Scale* is a design/prototype source, not a validated learning-outcomes trial.

## F. Provisional research-design implications (not an amended protocol)

1. **Preserve process evidence:** When ethically permitted, compare initial reasoning, feedback/AI exchange and revised reasoning plus a brief account of **why** specific suggestions were accepted, adapted or rejected. Do not conflate verbatim similarity with unthinking acceptance.
2. **Observe the social sequence:** Record whether a challenge came from the learner, peer, educator or AI, and whether it changed the *reasons given*. An AI-first/human-first comparison would need adequate process consistency and approved implementation.
3. **Make policy judgement visible:** Candidate observational codes include frame shift, causal claim, evidence warrant, uncertainty disclosure, option diversity, criteria/trade-offs, institutional legitimacy/operational capacity, justified acceptance, justified resistance and explicit delegation. **These are pilot codes** pending literature/theory review and feasibility testing.
4. **Triangulate without reducing learning to one proxy:** Elicited reflection, interaction traces, peer observations, artefacts and later application can each answer different questions. A later no-AI task can contribute evidence, but is not definitive by itself.
5. **Protect the protocol boundary:** The approved UCL *Practice as software* research plan currently specifies written seminar work, optional shared tool conversations and final memos; it explicitly excludes interviews/questionnaires/audio/video. Observation protocols, interviews and questionnaires are **developing amendment components** and require approval before use. Do not place participant research data into this public workspace or any AI analysis workflow contrary to approved arrangements.

## J. Foundational theory and critique: decision 5 (first pass, 7 November 2026)

**Scope:** This strand is not a test of AI credibility. It asks how learners develop the capability to make and account for situated judgements through work, dialogue, feedback and critique. Older primary theory is appropriate because its concepts predate and need not mention GenAI. We distinguish theoretical proposals, observational work and experimental outcome evidence.

### J1. Tai, Ajjawi, Boud, Dawson & Panadero (2018) — **full-text original conceptual article read; priority anchor**

*Developing evaluative judgement: enabling students to make decisions about the quality of work*. *Higher Education* 76, 467–481. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10734-017-0220-3. First online 16 December 2017; 2018 journal volume. **Access:** complete open-access publisher HTML, not just an abstract.

**Central argument:** Evaluative judgement is a person's capability to judge the quality of their own and others' work. The authors position it as an educational end in its own right rather than a side effect of receiving feedback or a final grade. It is socially and disciplinarily situated, developed through repeated encounters with exemplars, standards, peer and self-assessment, and feedback that helps learners judge quality rather than merely act on a mark (sections ‘Tracing the evolution’, ‘Current practices’ and ‘Conclusions’). They argue that the quality of a judgement includes the criteria invoked and the justification offered, not only the final decision.

**Method/status:** **Conceptual/theoretical synthesis, not an experimental effect study.** The authors explicitly acknowledge a need for longitudinal empirical tests. It would be wrong to say the article proves that peer critique *causes* better evaluative judgement. It gives a promising **construct and rationale**, not a validated PolicyMemo.ai measurement instrument.

**Use in our synthesis:** This is closer to the policy-reasoning research question than generic AI error-spotting scales. Tentative analytic attention: what counts as good policy work, how those standards become articulated, whether people can explain why a contribution is useful or inadequate, and how their criteria evolve. Distinguish *developing judgement* from *defending a decision persuasively*; verbal competence can be a polished retrospective account and not necessarily reveal the actual process.

### J3. Studio critique original literature — **full conceptual paper; empirical lead in original full text; additional leads**

**Oh, Ishizaki, Gross & Do (2013), *A theoretical framework of design critiquing in architecture studios*, *Design Studies* 34(3), 302–325.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.destud.2012.08.004. **Full publicly accessible PDF read**, https://collegiateteachinginartanddesign.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/theoretical-framework-for-design-studio-critique.pdf. This article synthesises prior studio-critique research into **11 proposed factors**, grouped into five *methods* (setting, teacher–student relationship, communication modality, delivery type and delivery) and six *conditions* (phase, individual differences, prior knowledge, student responses, artifacts and learning goals). It is a **descriptive conceptual framework**, not causal evidence that a particular studio activity improves learning (journal pp. 302–307, Table 1 p. 305). Its great relevance is methodological: the *setting and sequence of critique* are part of the phenomenon rather than nuisance variables.

**Goldschmidt, Hochman & Dafni (2010), *The design studio “crit”: Teacher–student communication*, *AI EDAM* 24(3), 285–302.** https://doi.org/10.1017/S089006041000020X. **Original article accessed in full text** via an author-posted/cached PDF at https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=d4dcb73d5a1a6323cf5d653f923ae6f962734cb3&repid=rep1&type=pdf . The article uses **three case studies of second-year architecture studio critiques**, with coding of verbal exchanges and linkography to study the relations among those exchanges (abstract; introduction, pp. 285–286). It demonstrates the *feasibility of studying feedback interaction rather than only final artefacts*. Three studio cases cannot establish generalised learning effects across disciplines or that a particular crit technique caused learning. This is a **primary empirical observational study** and warrants fuller appraisal if selected for the narrative review.

### J4. Initial comparison and implications — hypotheses, not final findings

1. **Judgement as an exercised capability (Tai) versus a product-quality score (Wiles).** Better assisted performance may show that a system is effective for a task. Whether the learner gains evaluative judgement requires looking at their decisions and rationale within disciplinary standards. The two outcomes are related but non-equivalent.
2. **Judgement as social practice (studio critique) versus an individual attribute.** Studio research suggests that reasoning is elicited, negotiated and sometimes transformed in dialogue around a provisional artefact. In the UCL setting, a student, peer, tutor and tool may contribute different challenges; task order matters as a feature to observe, but no evidence reviewed yet establishes the best sequence.
3. **Critique may produce learning or performance of compliance.** A reflective explanation may demonstrate developing judgement, or it may be a polished, socially acceptable rationalisation. Compare live interaction, revisions and participants' accounts when ethically permitted. Do not make an interview account the sole ground truth of a process.
4. **Context is essential.** Architecture studios, generic knowledge-work data science and postgraduate policy analysis differ in their values, responsibilities and professional standards. Transfer *conceptual mechanisms and methodological approaches*, not effects or populations, without argument.

### A-24: mark validity, not a claim of unadjusted marking

The researcher raised a legitimate question: if AI assistance is permitted in one exam condition, do the assessment and marking criteria still measure the same construct? The *abstract* reports assisted and unassisted exam scores, post-exam self-reported usage and verification, and performance improvements. It **does not specify whether marking rubrics were changed**. It would be incorrect to record unadjusted marking as a demonstrated flaw.

Keep A-24 excluded on scope grounds: it primarily measures assisted examination performance and self-reported verification, not the development or exercise of judgement over time. Preserve the assessment-validity question as a *cross-study appraisal flag* for other performance experiments: (i) equivalence of tasks, (ii) marking criteria and construct being assessed, (iii) presence or absence of AI in the outcome task, (iv) follow-up/transfer and (v) whether inference is about output, situated performance or learning.

### Contextual does not mean empirically strong

- **A-01:** Relevant account of learner reperformance and reviewing a make-or-buy decision. Its surveyed enthusiasm does **not** establish that professional judgement improved.
- **C-09:** Accounts of professionals' redistributed work and accountability. The review will use this only where it addresses judgement or work practice, not as an invitation to survey AI misinformation research.
- **C-21:** Provides a strategy-as-practice lens on situated professional discretion, but the study is about digitalisation broadly; AI-specific contribution may be limited.
- **A-17:** Remains uncertain pending whether its full questionnaire and analysis are informative about reflective behaviours. Statements that participants **believe** AI improves thinking must never be reported as demonstrated improvements.
- **B-25:** Researcher chose exclusion; its abstract lacks enough traceable detail on empirical interaction and judgement processes. This is a scoped pilot judgement, **not proof that the full paper contains no usable methods**.

## 4. Revised screening criteria (v0.2 — to test on remaining pilot records)

| Dimension | Operational guidance |
|---|---|
| **Central relevance** | Direct attention to the development, exercise or justification of judgement in complex tasks. Relevant activities include framing, reflective comparison, critique, informed revision, weighing alternatives, sense-making and human–AI–peer dialogue. |
| **Population** | Adult learners or professional practitioners are preferred; postgraduate status or prior professional experience is not mandatory. Closely comparable undergraduate or preservice teaching tasks may be retained, with a transferability caveat. |
| **Generative AI** | For *core empirical* status, the study should examine interaction with or use of GenAI in reasoning or professional work. A study of broad digitalisation without separable GenAI activity is contextual at most. |
| **Learning vs situated performance** | Extract and label what is measured: perceived learning, behaviour/process, quality of artefacts, task scores, independent capability/transfer, or longitudinal practice. Do not treat these as equivalent. Positive, negative and null findings are equally eligible. |
| **Core candidate** | Clear focus on *how* AI interaction mediates reasoning, reflection, critique, professional agency or decisions. Core does not mean high methodological quality; full-text appraisal determines evidentiary weight. |
| **Contextual candidate** | Important theory or adjacent empirical comparisons (including human-only feedback), mixed-technology professional practice, situated accountability or assessment design. Use selectively; do not expand into general credibility or AI ethics research. |
| **Uncertain** | Relevant topic but abstract does not establish whether methods or measures illuminate judgement; specifically flag 'perception-only' and inspect full text selectively. |
| **Exclude** | AI model accuracy benchmarks, general AI attitude/satisfaction surveys with no plausible process relevance, governance priorities without actual AI-supported practice, or generic teaching-technique catalogues that do not illuminate the central question. Reasons must be specific, not generic 'low quality'. |
| **Credibility boundary** | Verification and checking are **not automatically excluded** if part of evaluative judgement, accountability, reflection or decisions. Standalone studies of factual accuracy, misinformation spotting or trust calibration are outside the primary scope. |
| **Assessment validity flag** | For assisted/unassisted performance comparisons, check task equivalence, assessment rubrics, whether outputs rather than reasoning were graded, independent measures and delayed follow-up. Mark 'not reported in abstract' when unknown. |
| **Evidence weight** | Do not conflate screening relevance with causal inference. A six-person tool study may be core but exploratory; a large self-report survey may be contextual or uncertain. |

## 5. Further boundaries for the narrative synthesis

The likely organising concepts remain: practice and reflective learning; evaluative judgement; human–AI feedback and challenge; peer and teacher dialogue; delegation and professional agency; and the distinction among perceived development, situated capability and independent transfer.

The review should neither equate learning with detecting AI errors nor infer learning from better AI-assisted grades. It should also avoid assuming that all valuable learning must be demonstrated by immediate performance without AI.
