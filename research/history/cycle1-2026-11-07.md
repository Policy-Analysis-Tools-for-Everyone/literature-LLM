# PolicyMemo.ai — Critical narrative literature review

**Living evidence and synthesis | Cycle 1 | 7 November 2026 | Version 0.1 (pilot)**

**Status:** Working research document, *not* a completed literature review or a systematic review. This document is intended for further appraisal and critical revision. The accompanying workbook is `PolicyMemo-literature-review-log-cycle1-2026-11-07.xlsx`. **No formal database search has been conducted by this research team in this cycle.** Searches so far were targeted web searches and reference/citation leads, recorded in the workbook. Search counts are not reported where search interfaces did not provide reproducible totals. Search dates and search strings are recorded separately from the searches reported *by the reviewed authors*.

## A. Research scope and question (provisional)

**Primary review question:** How does interaction with generative AI shape the development, exercise and justification of judgement among adult learners and practitioners undertaking complex professional tasks?

**Situated question for the UCL study:** How do postgraduate policy students develop, exercise and explain judgement when working individually, with peers, with educators and with PolicyMemo.ai? Which observed differences can reasonably be interpreted as learning, as situated AI-assisted performance, or as redistributed judgement?

The second question does not assume that every postgraduate participant has substantial professional experience. Prior work experience is an empirical characteristic to establish, not an inclusion assumption.

**Design decision:** Keep this as a **focused, critical narrative review with a transparent pilot search and selection trail**. It is not a meta-analysis, systematic review or a claim of exhaustive coverage. The goal is explanatory synthesis that can inform study design and later publication, rather than aggregating incompatible proxies for critical thinking.

### A1. Search strands

1. **Professional learning, reflective practice and critique.** Reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action; feedback literacy and evaluative judgement; design studio critique; professional expertise, practice and transfer. Foundational texts can predate 2020 if conceptually necessary; they must be read in original form before quotation or detailed attribution.
2. **AI-mediated scaffolding, feedback and problem framing.** Prompting, coaching, challenge, alternate framings and feedback; design of tools that elicit reasoning rather than supply answers.
3. **Reliance, trust, agency and delegation.** Accept/reject/adapt decisions, justification, verification, calibrated reliance, automation bias, cognitive offloading, decision ownership.
4. **Individual–peer–teacher–AI interaction.** Reasoning as social practice, feedback and critique; task sequencing; peer discussion and distributed judgement; AI as a distinct participant in a learning ecology rather than an isolated treatment.
5. **Performance versus learning and transfer.** Quality of assisted output; reasoning process; later unaided performance; supported performance on novel tasks; retention, confidence and self-perception. These are *different* constructs and should not be collapsed.

### A2. Provisional pilot eligibility

- **Population:** Core: adult postgraduate learners, adult professionals or public-sector/policy practitioners doing complex analytical work. Adjacent: other university students whose tasks plausibly resemble policy reasoning; include only with explicit transferability caveats. K–12 evidence can illuminate mechanisms and tool design but is **not core population evidence**.
- **Intervention/exposure:** Conversational or generative AI, including domain-specific AI agents, chat interfaces and human–AI collaborative workflows. Non-generative automation can be used as theoretical context, not silently pooled with generative AI findings.
- **Relevant phenomena:** Problem framing, evidence evaluation, reasoning/argumentation, justified choices, feedback and critique, intellectual agency, reliance/offloading, or learning versus performance. Pure speed/productivity results are context unless linked to judgement or learning.
- **Methods:** Empirical original studies and substantive systematic/scoping reviews or meta-analyses; include qualitative and mixed-methods research. Foundational theory and design/position papers remain in a labelled *conceptual/design* stream, not in the empirical-effects stream.
- **Period:** Initial empirical search window 7 November 2020–7 November 2026, with emphasis on the post-November-2022 generative-AI period. Do not exclude foundational work on date alone.
- **Methodological relevance:** Record actual sample, intervention, comparator, independent versus assisted assessments, observation versus self-report, baseline equivalence, follow-up, conflicts of interest and construct validity. Negative/null findings are explicitly eligible.
- **Screening levels:** Core / adjacent / conceptual or context / deferred or exclude. Inclusion for narrative discussion is **not** endorsement of a study's causal claims.

**Trade-off:** A tight postgraduate-only criterion would miss comparative experiments on how AI scaffolding works; admitting every student-facing AI study would swamp professional judgement with language-learning and short task outcomes. Use tiered relevance plus methodological appraisal. In the eventual written review, separate evidence of effects on *scores* from evidence of mechanisms of *judgement*.

## C. First-batch critical appraisal: three starting papers

### C1. Deng et al. (2025), *Does ChatGPT enhance student learning? A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental studies*

**Citation:** Deng, R., Jiang, M., Yu, X., Lu, Y. & Liu, S. (2025). *Computers & Education*, 227, 105224. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105224. Full text: local uploaded `1-s2.0-S0360131524002380-main.pdf`.

**Question and scope.** What does experimental and quasi-experimental evidence say about ChatGPT's effects on different dimensions of student learning, and how do settings and methods shape reported effects? Search through **31 August 2024** in EBSCOhost, IEEE Xplore, PsycINFO, Scopus and Web of Science. PRISMA screening: 1,683 database records, 69 included articles; 62 contributed to quantitative meta-analyses. This is **the authors' database search**, not ours (PDF pp. 6–10, particularly p. 6 search description and p. 9 analysis).

**Population and interventions.** Educational studies, predominantly university students, with a range of subjects but an emphasis on language education and classroom use. Interventions involved ChatGPT, not all generative-AI systems or all agentic workflows. Designs include experimental/quasi-experimental group comparisons. The included participants and contexts are heterogeneous, not a focused sample of adult policy practitioners (pp. 10–12).

**Methods and appraisal.** The review used quantitative study-quality criteria (Kmet et al.), dual independent rating and inter-rater agreement; random-effects meta-analysis of Hedges' g. Positive feature: explicit search, eligibility, review process and sensitivity/bias analyses. Limitation: outcome families are broad, varied across fields; the review deliberately excluded qualitative and within-subject studies, which often illuminate *how* people reason. Heterogeneity across outcomes is exceptionally high; publication-bias tests flag potential issues for academic performance and affective/motivational outcomes (pp. 6–7, 9, 12–13).

**Findings (authors' pooled estimates, PDF p. 13, Table 7):** academic performance **g = 0.712** (95% CI 0.497–0.926; **I² = 91.8%**); affective-motivational states **g = 0.881** (I² = 92.9%); higher-order-thinking **propensities** **g = 0.703** (I² = 90.3%); self-efficacy **g = 0.441**, CI crossing zero; mental effort **g = −0.675**, based on only four effect sizes. These are not uniform effects on one stable construct.

**Crucial limitations.** For academic-performance studies, nine explicitly **allowed** ChatGPT during post-intervention assessment, while 33 did **not state whether it was allowed** (p. 18). This means assisted product quality can be mistaken for learning. The higher-order-thinking outcome was often *self-reported propensity/disposition*, not demonstrated performance (p. 20). Just **five of 62** meta-analysed studies reported power analysis; baseline differences were handled unevenly (pp. 15–16 and 21–22). Motivational benefits might reflect novelty; the authors note limited repeated measurement during the intervention (p. 19).

**Assessment of evidential weight.** **Moderate for the broad claim** that many studied interventions improve measured educational outcomes; **low for a claim about durable, independent policy judgement**. Very high heterogeneity should block translating a pooled g into an expected effect in PolicyMemo.ai.

**Implication for PolicyMemo.ai.** Keep *assisted artefact quality*, *justification/process*, *quality of policy judgement* and *learning over time* distinct. Prompt/AI traces alone cannot establish either improvement or deterioration. Do not equate lower mental effort with lower learning: offloading could remove low-value work or displace necessary practice.

**Citation leads:** Niloy et al. (2024) (negative originality outcome); original studies measuring unaided outcomes, multiweek interventions or follow-up; scrutinise samples, assessment conditions and whether critical thinking is self-reported.

### C2. Cong-Lem & Nguyen (2026), *Towards a framework for understanding and assessing critical thinking in generative AI-enhanced learning environments: a scoping review*

**Citation:** Cong-Lem, N. & Nguyen, T. T.-D. (2026). *European Journal of Psychology of Education*, 41, article 41. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10212-026-01089-y. Full text: uploaded `s10212-026-01089-y.pdf` (25 pages, article page numbering 1–25).

**Question and scope.** How has critical thinking (CT) been conceptualised and measured in GenAI-enhanced learning, and what impacts are reported? Structured review of empirical educational studies from November 2022 to January 2025; searches in Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar on 6 February 2025 yielded 1,991 records; **29** were included (pp. 3–7). This was **the authors' formal search**, not ours.

**Population and methods.** Primarily higher education: **27/29** university studies, but mixed undergraduate/postgraduate populations. Quantitative (17), mixed methods (11) and qualitative (1). Two-reviewer title/abstract and full-text screening, Covidence workflow; Google Scholar screened only first 100 records. The Scopus query placed `AI/ChatGPT` and `critical thinking` in title fields and assessment-related terms in title/abstract/keywords, potentially missing relevant studies using judgement/decision language rather than *critical thinking* (pp. 4–9).

**Findings.** Three important contributions: (1) CT means different things in different papers, from reflective judgement to verification and detecting GenAI errors; (2) measurement ranges from self-report scales to contextual tasks; (3) authors classify **21/29** studies as positive, seven as mixed and one as negative (pp. 9–13). Their four-part proposed model is **analytical reasoning, source verification, metacognitive self-regulation and CT dispositions** (pp. 13–15).

**Crucial limitations.** A **scoping review without formal study-level quality/risk-of-bias appraisal** (p. 16). Counting 21 positive studies does not mean there is a 72.4% probability that AI improves CT, nor that the positive studies are the strongest. The title-dependent search and restriction to peer-reviewed journals may underrepresent professional judgement, negative/null results, conference HCI research and post-January-2025 studies. Its proposed model is conceptually useful but not thereby a validated predictive theory (pp. 4–6, 16).

**Assessment of evidential weight.** **High usefulness for construct mapping; limited evidential weight for a causal effect estimate.** Avoid writing that the four dimensions have been empirically proven to cause learning; that overreads a synthesis proposing a framework.

**Implication for PolicyMemo.ai.** Treat verification, justification, metacognitive regulation and ownership of judgement as *possible observables*, not a compulsory four-item checklist. In particular, students can rationally accept accurate AI contributions; identifying an error is not the sole sign of critical thinking. Add attention to policy-specific normative judgement, contestable values and institutional/political feasibility—elements not guaranteed by generic CT scales.

**Citation leads:** Essien et al. (2024; UK postgraduates); de la Puente et al. (2024; international-relations debates); guidance-based instructional designs and explicit individual/peer/AI sequences (specific originals to verify before citing as evidence). The review explicitly excludes workplace/professional studies outside educational settings (p. 6): a **known search blind spot** for our review.

### C3. Casey (2026), *ChatGPT in public policy teaching and assessment: An examination of opportunities and challenges*

**Citation:** Casey, D. (2026 issue; accepted 2024). *Australian Journal of Public Administration*, 85, 362–376. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8500.12647. Full text: uploaded `Aust J Public Adm - 2024 - Casey - ... .pdf` (15 pages; publisher pages 362–376).

**Question and setting.** How do policy students understand ChatGPT in teaching/assessment, and its potential use in policy formulation? A **Bachelor of Public Policy capstone** at the Australian National University, not postgraduate policy training. All 24 enrolled students used ChatGPT to draft a decision brief and then reflected; **seven of 24 consented** to research use of their essays and transcripts, and **five** attended a later focus group. Qualitative inductive thematic coding; ChatGPT was also used to assist coding/themes (pp. 366–368 and appendix p. 376).

**Findings.** Participants reported recognising weak nuance, problems with source transparency, insufficient consideration of politics, governance and fairness; some saw the exercise as useful for AI literacy and critique/evaluative judgement. The paper suggests tasks requiring deliberate analysis of AI output, not just its production, have educational promise. Student scepticism regarding policy use was salient (pp. 367–370).

**Crucial limitations.** Tiny volunteer sample in one university and a **deliberately artificial task** requiring ChatGPT to produce the brief, using the 2023 free ChatGPT 3.5 generation; students' reports cannot demonstrate durable improvements in reasoning. Participation is self-selected; perceptions may be shaped by briefing, grading and the intentionally constrained exercise. Researcher-teacher dual role and ChatGPT-assisted qualitative coding warrant a transparent interpretive audit (pp. 366–367, 370–371).

**Assessment of evidential weight.** **High contextual relevance to policy reasoning; low causal weight for learning gains.** This is an instructive qualitative case, not a demonstration that critique training enhances independent judgement.

**Implication for PolicyMemo.ai.** Use policy-domain-specific coding of *why* an output was adapted or rejected: problem diagnosis, strength of evidence, competing values, institutional feasibility, uncertainty and authoriser context. Give space for students to **defend a warranted acceptance** of a tool suggestion, not just report its mistakes. Consider eliciting their judgement before and after AI, with reasoning notes and peer discussion context; any new data collection must be ethics-approved.

## D. Original-study pilot: first-pass appraisal and critical counter-evidence

The following records are original studies, not additional systematic reviews. Their relevance tiers and source-depth are intentional; **no numerical effect from these studies is pooled**.

### D1. Lee et al. (2025): professional critical thinking as stewardship — **core; full author PDF**

**Reference:** Lee, H.-P. et al. (2025), *The impact of generative AI on critical thinking: Self-reported reductions in cognitive effort and confidence effects from a survey of knowledge workers*, *CHI '25*, article 1121. https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778; author PDF: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf.

**Question/method/population:** Survey of **319** knowledge workers recruited through Prolific who reported using GenAI at work weekly; **936** detailed, recalled workplace task examples. Quantitative models of **self-reported** enactment and effort associated with CT; qualitative coding of examples. Participants had varied jobs, so not all have policy expertise or verified professional seniority (full PDF §§3–6).

**Findings:** Greater task-specific confidence in AI is associated with reduced perceived CT effort/enactment; greater self-confidence is associated with more. Workers describe shifts **from information gathering to verification**, **from execution to response integration**, and **from task execution to stewardship**. Their own discussion warns that reduced effort may represent useful support, offloading, less CT, or a confusion between mental effort and CT effort (§§4–6).

**Appraisal:** Particularly good **conceptual and ecological proximity** to professional work, and unusually sensitive to altered *forms* of judgement. But a single cross-sectional retrospective survey cannot causally measure cognitive decline, skill transfer or actual effort; users self-select into examples and assess their own CT (limitations §6.3). **Usefulness: high for mechanisms and interview vocabulary, low for causal learning effects.**

### D2. Essien et al. (2024): postgraduate education, but compromised comparison — **core; full publisher PDF text**

**Reference:** Essien, A. E., Bukoye, O. T., O'Dea, X. & Kremantzis, M. D. (2024), *The influence of AI text generators on critical thinking skills in UK business schools*, *Studies in Higher Education* 49(5), 865–882. https://doi.org/10.1080/03075079.2024.2316881. Full publisher PDF: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/03075079.2024.2316881.

**Question/method/population:** A reported **107 UK postgraduate business students** in a mixed-method/quasi-experimental design framed around Bloom's taxonomy; researchers discuss control and ChatGPT intervention groups. **Crucial detail:** the methods explicitly state that the *post-test was conducted exclusively with the experimental group* (publisher pp. 869–870, §3.1). Authors report bigger changes at lower Bloom levels than higher ones, and raise reliability and accuracy concerns.

**Appraisal:** Population is highly comparable. However, the reported one-group follow-up undermines the ability to estimate a treatment-versus-control difference after exposure, even if groups were divided at baseline; paired pre/post improvement may reflect maturation, repeated assessment or other exposures. There are also complexities in matching reported sample and statistical tables that need detailed verification. **Do not cite this as strong causal evidence of AI improving postgraduate CT.** Use as a useful *case of outcome-measure and design ambiguity*, with high setting relevance but low causal certainty.

### D3. de la Puente et al. (2024): policy-adjacent debate, but credibility caution — **adjacent; full publisher HTML**

**Reference:** de la Puente, M., Torres, J., Blanco Troncoso, A. L. et al. (2024), *Investigating the use of ChatGPT as a tool for enhancing critical thinking and argumentation skills in international relations debates among undergraduate students*, *Smart Learning Environments* 11, 55. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40561-024-00347-0.

**Question/method/population:** **95 undergraduate** international-relations students in Colombia, randomly allocated **48** to AI-assisted debate and **47** to conventional debate; authors report pre/post measures of conceptual understanding, CT and argumentation, with favourable group comparisons. This is closer to policy-style argument than ordinary vocabulary tutoring. The intervention also included **fact-checking, peer debate, feedback and instructor scaffolding**, so it tests a **package** rather than isolated AI exposure (publisher HTML, Research method, Results).

**Appraisal and red flag:** The original full article contains **anachronistic summaries of older research**—for example, attributing use of ChatGPT to Kuhn and Udell (2003), decades before the tool existed. That is a concrete citation-handling defect in the article's literature review and warrants closer checking of methods, coding and cited prior findings. It does **not logically prove that its trial data are invalid**, but the study should not be elevated to strong causal evidence until the original measurement and protocol details are checked carefully. Other limits: undergraduates not postgraduates; possible bundled instruction effects; uncertain transfer to autonomous policy judgement. **Current status: adjacent/conditional, not a headline finding.**

### D4. Bastani et al. (2025): contrary findings from guided versus unguided AI — **mechanism only; full PMC HTML**

**Reference:** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A. et al. (2025), *Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics*, *PNAS* 122(26), e2422633122. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122. Full HTML: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/.

**Method/population:** Large randomised classroom field trial involving nearly 1,000 **high-school mathematics students**, comparing a generic GPT interface, a safeguarded tutor designed to guide without simply supplying answers, and no AI; assisted practice and immediately following **unaided** exam.

**Findings:** Both AI conditions increased assisted practice scores; generic GPT users scored **17% below controls** on the subsequent unaided exam, while the safeguarded condition was **not detectably different** from control on that exam. This is a **short-term, specific maths outcome**, not direct evidence about adult public-policy reasoning or long-term professional judgement.

**Appraisal:** Stronger design for distinguishing assisted task performance from immediate unaided performance; plausible mechanism via answer-copying patterns. But outside core age group and domain and not longitudinal transfer. **Retain as carefully qualified counter-evidence for intervention design**, not as an estimate for the UCL population.

### D5. Wiles et al. (2026): temporary upskilling of consultants — **core, abstract only; priority full-text retrieval**

**Reference:** Wiles, E., Krayer, L., Abbadi, M. et al. (2026), *Generative AI and the temporary upskilling of knowledge workers*, *Nature Human Behaviour*, first published **26 October 2026**. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-026-02566-6. Publisher abstract and PubMed record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42934398/.

**What can be stated:** Abstract reports an RCT with **487** management consultants using ChatGPT on data-science tasks outside their expertise; treatment raised assisted performance on three tasks but did **not provide evidence of improved unassisted technical-question performance** immediately afterwards. **Not yet a full-text appraisal**: cannot responsibly judge primary measures, confidence intervals, comparisons or instructional context until the publisher PDF or UCL access copy is obtained.

**Caveat:** Co-authors include BCG and OpenAI researchers and BCG supplied access/resources; this is a disclosed interest, not grounds for automatic exclusion. Assess independence, pre-analysis plan and the metric's construct validity on full-text reading.

### D6. Niloy et al. (2024): negative originality finding — **adjacent, full text still needed**

**Reference:** Niloy, A. C., Akter, S., Sultana, N. et al. (2024), *Is ChatGPT a menace for creative writing ability? An experiment*, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* 40(2), 919–930. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12929. ## E. Emerging synthesis — hypotheses to test, not established conclusions

**E1. AI can redistribute effort, not simply add/subtract thinking.** Lee documents changes in the *location* of judgement (verification, integration, stewardship), while Deng aggregates broad improvements in conventional outcome scores. Neither independently proves a net gain or loss in learning. Some offloading may be an appropriate division of labour; whether it is educationally harmful depends on which cognitive activity is outsourced and why.

**E2. Who remains epistemically accountable?** Cong-Lem & Nguyen's framework emphasizes analytical reasoning, verification and self-regulation; Casey's policy case highlights contextual, political and normative adequacy. In policy work, a technically correct option may still be inappropriate because it ignores authorisation, power, stakeholders or public value. Judgement should be observed in *warranting* choices, not simply correcting factual errors.

**E3. Critique is socially situated.** Casey uses an AI brief as a common object for critique, but did not isolate the roles of peers, teacher and AI. The de la Puente intervention bundles AI with debate and peer feedback. Our study should observe which party introduced a reframing or challenge and how it was negotiated, not automatically attribute a revised memo to AI.

**E4. Assistance and skill acquisition can diverge.** Deng's assessments often do not separate tool use from learner ability. Wiles (abstract) and Bastani (different age/domain) test a sharper boundary and find limited or adverse unaided outcomes. But *an unaided task alone is not exhaustive evidence of learning*: learning may include stronger supported judgement, better questions, improved collaboration or appropriate delegation. More than one outcome is needed.

**E5. Study design needs a mechanism, not a predetermined victory story.** A credible qualitative study should be able to identify productive AI scaffolding, justified refusal, justified acceptance, premature closure, superficial copying, productive offloading, peer-mediated correction and no change. 'Detects an AI error' is a possible indicator—not the master definition of CT. 'Produced a better memo' is a possible outcome—not proof of learning.

### Explicit uncertainties and gaps

- What does **policy-specific judgement** comprise beyond generic critical-thinking scales: framing contested problems, evaluating evidence/uncertainty, recognising trade-offs, handling legitimacy and feasibility, and defending proposals to authorisers?
- Few mapped educational reviews include **actual workplace/professional** populations or recent **agent-based workflows**. A focused professional-study strand is necessary.
- Comparisons of **AI-first versus human/peer-first sequencing** need direct empirical studies. The current first batch does not demonstrate which ordering is best.
- **Experienced versus less experienced adult learners** might use AI differently; do not presume experience from postgraduate enrolment.
- Self-report, observable trace, assisted quality and independent application require distinct measurement strategies and analytic claims.
- We have not yet closely read Schön's books or original design-studio critique studies. Jon Kolko's essays are potential routes to originals, not original empirical evidence for this review. Mollick et al.'s 2024 *Simulated Practice at Scale* is a design/prototype source, not a validated learning-outcomes trial.

## F. Provisional research-design implications (not an amended protocol)

1. **Preserve process evidence:** When ethically permitted, compare initial reasoning, feedback/AI exchange and revised reasoning plus a brief account of **why** specific suggestions were accepted, adapted or rejected. Do not conflate verbatim similarity with unthinking acceptance.
2. **Observe the social sequence:** Record whether a challenge came from the learner, peer, educator or AI, and whether it changed the *reasons given*. An AI-first/human-first comparison would need adequate process consistency and approved implementation.
3. **Make policy judgement visible:** Candidate observational codes include frame shift, causal claim, evidence warrant, uncertainty disclosure, option diversity, criteria/trade-offs, institutional legitimacy/operational capacity, justified acceptance, justified resistance and explicit delegation. **These are pilot codes** pending literature/theory review and feasibility testing.
4. **Triangulate without reducing learning to one proxy:** Elicited reflection, interaction traces, peer observations, artefacts and later application can each answer different questions. A later no-AI task can contribute evidence, but is not definitive by itself.
5. **Protect the protocol boundary:** The approved UCL *Practice as software* research plan currently specifies written seminar work, optional shared tool conversations and final memos; it explicitly excludes interviews/questionnaires/audio/video. Observation protocols, interviews and questionnaires are **developing amendment components** and require approval before use. Do not place participant research data into this public workspace or any AI analysis workflow contrary to approved arrangements.
