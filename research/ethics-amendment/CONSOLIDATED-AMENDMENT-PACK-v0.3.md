## Submission context and confirmed selections

- **Research approach:** Model C, policy judgement in practice. Examine making and justifying policy decisions, peer/tutor and AI influences, and cautiously any later changes in practice.
- **Teaching:** two core PolicyMemo.ai sessions — Moore's Strategic Triangle and Thinking Hats; normal formative-feedback and other teaching remain primarily human-led. A third AI seminar is optional and not part of the default protocol.
- **Formative research materials:** request **both** the student's **original, ungraded, 1,000-word formative problem definition and analysis**, and the **tutor's written feedback** as **two distinct optional permissions**, rather than assuming access under the original consent for seminar worksheets.
- **Timing:** syllabus due date of formative is 28 October 2026, preceding planned PolicyMemo.ai teaching according to researcher. **Check actual first use by each group.** Some may already have used ChatGPT, Claude or other generic AI tools. Ask in the first research interview about their actual use/non-use; do **not** describe this as an AI-free baseline.
- **Two interview waves:** roughly 6–8 participant volunteers interviewed in-module by an independent interviewer, then after final grades, with a separately optional unfamiliar individual task. Real cohort timings and availability require confirmation.
- **Assessment and independence:** marking researcher does not know consent identities and receives no research data until marks are final. Consent/recruitment is independently administered. Retention and withdrawal timing must be reviewed for new follow-up.
- **Analysis:** human-first thematic/comparative qualitative interpretation and second-human critique. An LLM may challenge non-identifying aggregate researcher analyses only as institutionally permitted; any processing of student text/transcripts requires explicit further UCL ethics and data/security authorisation. **Redacting names is not automatically anonymisation**.

## Proposed amendment summary — ready to adapt into the committee form

The proposed amendment develops the approved *Practice as software* study into a small longitudinal qualitative investigation of how postgraduate policy students make, scrutinise and justify policy-analysis choices when independent thought, teaching feedback, peer critique and AI-supported learning intersect. A critical narrative review of literature on evaluative judgement, metacognition, feedback uptake and professional practice motivates distinguishing improved assisted written work from the judgement students exercise in producing it and from evidence that those capabilities change over time.

The main addition is **specific consent to include students' original formative problem definitions and analysis alongside the tutors' written feedback as separate sources**. The formative is a dated early account of the student's reasoning, submitted before their reported introduction to PolicyMemo.ai, but not necessarily completed without general AI assistance. A first research interview will therefore ask what tools, if any, students used, when, why and how they assessed any suggestions; claims about historic AI use will be reported as self-reported unless separately substantiated. The tutor's feedback represents a further input against which students may evaluate, adapt or reject advice. The study will not treat provision of feedback as evidence it was taken up.

Two specified seminar formats would include PolicyMemo.ai as a **critical reader only after individual and peer work**: Moore's Strategic Triangle (public value, legitimacy and operational capacity) and Thinking Hats policy memo peer review (contrasting critiques and reasoned selection of useful advice). Other seminars need not use PolicyMemo.ai, offering context about reasoning and feedback without a live tool. Additional procedures proposed for approval are consent-sensitive structured observation and two optional semi-structured interviews with a nested sample of approximately six to eight students, one in-module and one after grades. The latter may include a separate unfamiliar short individual task, under defined assistance conditions. An adequately comparable earlier task would be needed to make stronger claims about change in independent reasoning; the existing formative is a different assignment type and cannot automatically serve that purpose.

Participation and each data source remain optional; non-consenting students receive equivalent teaching. Independent staff hold consent and contact information, and any in-module interviews, while the marking researcher remains blind to participant identities and materials until assessment is completed. The original plan explicitly excluded interviews, questionnaires, recording, individual observation and AI processing of research data, so these activities **must not commence without amendment approval and any required information-security/data-protection clearance**. Participant data will remain in authorised UCL systems, with specific arrangements for indirect identification and any eventual publication quotations. The study is qualitative and not a randomised or causal test of PolicyMemo.ai. New arrangements for recruitment, contact after grading, withdrawal, retention, tutor feedback ownership and AI processing must be finalised before submission.

# Revised research plan — proposed amendment v0.3 (formative submission + written feedback)

**Study:** *Practice as software: exploring the use of AI in policy reasoning and critical thinking*  
**Setting:** IIPP0010 postgraduate policy-analysis teaching, UCL IIPP.  
**Status:** Working revision for researcher and sponsor review; **not submitted or approved**. Builds on v0.1 and a newly identified pre-PolicyMemo.ai formative assignment opportunity. The original approved plan remains authoritative until permission is confirmed.

## Purpose and research question

The study examines **when and how students make, scrutinise and justify consequential policy-analysis choices in AI-supported practice**, and what evidence, if any, suggests changes in that capability over time. Rather than treating stronger written work or more frequent AI use as evidence of learning, it investigates how students assess problems, evidence, uncertainties, options and trade-offs, and how their decisions are shaped by individual thought, peer exchange, teaching feedback and PolicyMemo.ai.

**Subsidiary questions**
1. How do students make and explain policy judgements before, during and after interaction with other students and AI?
2. What do students accept, adapt, reject or question in feedback, and what reasons support those responses?
3. How do students describe their AI working practices and the conditions under which they use or decline the tool?
4. How did students approach AI use (or deliberate non-use) when preparing their initial formative assignment **before exposure to PolicyMemo.ai**, and how does their later reported practice differ after interacting with the tool?
5. What does later individual work suggest about the continued exercise of policy judgement, distinct from better performance during the supported module?

**Conceptual framing:** policy judgement is the substantive object; evaluative judgement helps examine quality appraisal, metacognition and reflective practice sensitise analysis of monitoring/reframing, and a practice perspective attends to students, peers, teaching methods and AI. These are complementary perspectives, **not four psychometric scales**. [Saved framing](../../wiki/synthesis/theoretical-perspectives-writing-note.md).

## Design

Small **longitudinal qualitative study embedded in authentic postgraduate teaching**. Proposed recruitment remains voluntary, with a potential expansion from the original two seminar groups **only if the amendment approves it**.

**Teaching:** two deliberately bounded PolicyMemo.ai teaching activities are proposed: (A) Moore's Strategic Triangle, where AI critiques the students' account of public value, legitimacy and capacity after independent and peer reasoning; and (B) Thinking Hats memo feedback, where AI acts as a final critical reader after successive peer-review rounds. The ordinary formative-feedback seminar and other teaching remain non-AI-led. The optional third imperfect-evidence AI activity is **not a default required element**. See [two-seminar framework](../design/two-ai-seminar-framework-v0.5.md). Non-AI peer alternatives remain available to all students, irrespective of study consent.

## Formative assignment as a natural starting point

The formative assignment is the student's **initial policy problem statement and problem analysis**, submitted **before students had used PolicyMemo.ai**. It provides an authentic, dated account of how a student defined the issue, reasoned about its causes and selected initial evidence. It can anchor later interviews and comparisons with subsequent seminar work and the final policy memo.

**Proposed research materials (both agreed for inclusion in the amendment):** seek **separate optional consent** for (1) the student's **original, submitted formative problem definition and analysis** and (2) the **written tutor feedback on that submission**. Both are educational records created before this amended research plan; neither may be harvested automatically from Moodle or transferred to the researcher merely because the student joined other parts of the study. Provide an independent UCL-approved submission/release route, access the materials for analysis only **after marks are finalised**, and check any staff authorship/confidentiality considerations for feedback. The originally approved plan refers to *seminar work* rather than these separately submitted assessment records.

**Critical design distinction:** this is a **pre-PolicyMemo.ai** starting point, **not an AI-free baseline**. Some students may have used generic generative-AI tools (for example ChatGPT, Claude or Copilot), while others may have used none. The initial interview should establish **whether, when, how and why** students used those tools (or chose not to) in preparing the formative. Where recall is uncertain, code the answer as uncertain rather than infer use from writing style. Prior tool exposure, familiarity and perceived confidence can help interpret subsequent experiences, without ranking students as skilled/unskilled solely from self-report.

**Interview prompts anchored in the actual assignment** (to be incorporated into the interview guide *after this research plan is agreed*):
- “Walk me through how you developed the problem definition and analysis for your formative assignment. Where did you begin?”
- “Did you use an AI tool at any point? Which tool or tools, if you remember? At what stages, and for what purpose?”
- “What did you ask the AI to do—generate possible causes, challenge assumptions, suggest sources, draft wording, or something else? How did you decide what to keep, change or reject?”
- “If you did not use AI, was that a deliberate choice? What shaped it?”
- “Were any claims, sources or framings particularly difficult to judge? How did you assess them?”
- **After exploring the original formative practice, and only then:** “How does that approach compare with the way you work now, including any experience of PolicyMemo.ai?”

This is valuable **retrospective evidence about practice and perceived change**, but an interview cannot verify every historic AI interaction. AI chat logs from **other providers** would be a **further optional and separately governed data class**, not automatically covered by the original permission to share selected *PolicyMemo.ai* conversations. Do not request those logs until the ethics and data-handling basis is clear.

**Analytical comparison:** (i) formative problem analysis and students' accounts of general AI or non-AI use; (ii) later peer/tutor and PolicyMemo.ai challenges; (iii) final memo and the reasons students can provide for key changes; and (iv) a later unfamiliar task where ethically approved. Differences may reflect teaching, task development, feedback, experience, self-selection or a changed tool—not simply the causal effect of PolicyMemo.ai. The formative is **not automatically comparable** to a later independent problem-solving task because the task requirements differ.

**Possible research material:** separately consented **original formative assignment and, under a distinct permission, its written tutor feedback**; existing seminar written work; voluntarily shared selected PolicyMemo.ai conversations; final Personal Policy Problem memos **only after marks are final**; and non-identifying group-level teaching notes, consistent with the approved baseline. Newly collected formative assessments/feedback, interview accounts and other procedures must be clearly justified and permissioned in the amendment.

**Additional methods proposed (not authorised yet):**
- Structured, consent-sensitive observations of selected seminar activities, preferably by an independent observer, separating observed actions from interpretation.
- Purposive longitudinal interviews with approximately **six to eight adult students**, two interviews per participant: one during the module (twenty to thirty minutes) and another after grades are released (thirty to forty-five minutes, plus an optional later task). Twelve to sixteen interviews if all complete both. Sampling should include different **reported formative AI-use patterns, including non-use**, and later experiences with PolicyMemo.ai where feasible, without collecting unnecessary demographics. These are qualitative contextual variations, not a powered comparison of generic AI versus PolicyMemo.ai.
- A **short unfamiliar individual policy task** approximately six weeks after teaching, and only after grades have been released, to examine how participants approach a comparable problem under stated support conditions. An earlier genuinely comparable individual activity is needed before claiming development; if none exists, report the follow-up as later performance and reflection, not change attributable to AI.

**Timing:** the formative assignment was due on **28 October 2026** according to the 2026–27 module syllabus. The researcher states that the formative predates students' introduction to PolicyMemo.ai; confirm the actual tool introduction dates by group before calling every formative pre-tool. It must be collected only retrospectively with approved voluntary consent; this does not permit researchers to reconstruct an objectively observed pre-AI usage record. The actual seminar delivery dates and teaching/assessment timetable must be confirmed; by 14 November 2026 some proposed seminar types have already occurred. No retroactive research activity, retrospective 'baseline' or unapproved research collection is authorised by this draft. The original 31 March 2027 withdrawal date may conflict with follow-up; agree revised dates, retention and consent with UCL before submission.

## Research procedures and safeguards

All students receive equivalent teaching regardless of consent or tool use. Recruitment and consent are administered independently of the tutor/marker. **Collecting formative coursework and tutor feedback is voluntary and not linked to assessment outcomes; the independent custodian holds permissions, and the teaching researcher receives neither attributable work nor consent identities before grades are finalised.** **A mid-module interview requires an independent interviewer**, with transcripts and participation identities unavailable to the teaching researcher until assessment is complete, consistent with amendment approval. Participation, withdrawal and criticism of PolicyMemo.ai cannot affect marks. No unconsented students' individual speech, writing or AI prompts enter research data.

Interviews can be audio-recorded **only with a specific optional recording permission** and approved UCL collection/transcription tools; otherwise written contemporaneous notes may be used if specifically approved. Follow-up contact is separately opt-in. Avoid requesting sensitive/confidential policy or workplace data.

## Analysis

Human researchers work first from approved, pseudonymised materials in access-controlled UCL storage. Analyse **problem definition, evidence, uncertainty, options/trade-offs and justification/judgement**, allowing inductive findings and deliberate search for contradictory cases. Compare documented earlier/later positions, explanations of changes and non-changes, formative reported generic AI use/non-use versus later PolicyMemo.ai practices, AI feedback offered versus student uptake, and student accounts with classroom/artefact evidence. **Record reported tool use separately from verified traces**, and compare common reasoning dimensions rather than equating differences in formative and final assignment requirements with learning. A second human researcher reviews a purposive sample and disagreements are recorded; the small qualitative sample is not a causal or powered AI-effect trial. Do not equate improvement in an AI-supported memo with retained independent learning.

**LLM-assisted analytical critique — conditional proposal:** The original approval expressly bars putting student research data into any AI tool. Our preferred primary method remains **human-only**. We may separately request permission to have a suitable institutionally approved LLM challenge non-identifying **researcher-written summaries, analytic claims and synthetic examples**. Direct participant excerpts or transcripts, even name-redacted, would require additional explicit data protection, information-security and ethics authorisation for the particular service. A real name removed does **not** ensure anonymity. Any AI suggestion is verified by the researchers against protected originals and the accept/reject decision logged. This is not a replacement for human coding.

## Intended interpretation, limitations and contribution

A qualitative, situated account of how students exercise policy judgement with people and tools; what seems to encourage scrutiny or uncritical adoption of feedback; and whether later work/participant accounts are consistent with changing practice. Rival explanations include policy teaching, peer challenge, repeated task exposure, existing ability, language proficiency, opt-in bias, AI provider differences, assessment incentives and the researcher-developer role. The study should inform pedagogy and responsible AI-tool design, without overstating causal effectiveness or independent skill growth.

## Review notes — 14 November 2026

**Decision confirmed:** request both original formative coursework and written tutor feedback as separate optional research materials. Their purpose differs: the former reveals early **problem framing and causal analysis**; the latter records an external critique against which students may exercise judgement. Where actual student uptake of feedback is unknown, infer nothing from its presence alone.

**Comparison limits:** course feedback can encourage improvement independently of AI, and later tasks expect policy options that the formative did not require. Analyse shared dimensions—framing, evidence, assumptions and uncertainty—rather than comparing the assignment marks (the formative is ungraded). The initial interview will cover prior use/non-use of other AI tools, recognising recall limitations.

# Semi-structured interview guide — proposed amendment v0.2

**Status:** for ethics review, not approved collection. **Proposed sample:** six to eight consented students, purposively varied as far as appropriate, with two interviews each. The original plan authorised **no research interviews**.

## Ground rules for both interviews

Read the approved information sheet. Remind participants: voluntary, unrelated to marks, skip questions, stop at any time, do not reveal confidential workplace/third-party information. Record only with separate consent and UCL-approved arrangements. **Mid-module interviewer must be independent of teaching/marking** and must prevent disclosure of identity/responses to the marker before grades are final.

Interviews should explore **specific examples** and invite disagreement. They are interpretive accounts, not direct observations of unrecorded thinking.

## Wave 1 — during the module (twenty to thirty minutes)

**Opening — begin with the formative, before discussion of PolicyMemo.ai:** “Take me back to the original formative policy problem definition and analysis. How did you approach it and how did you decide what the central problem and its causes were?”

**Previous AI use, including non-use:** “Did you use any AI tools when preparing that formative? Which ones, if you remember, and what did you ask them to do?” Follow up neutrally on problem framing, searching for evidence, exploring causes, challenging assumptions, drafting or editing. “How did you check what they told you? Were there suggestions you used, changed or rejected? If you didn't use AI, what influenced that choice?” **Do not request access to other providers' past chat transcripts in this core interview.**

**Formative feedback (only if separately available to the independent interviewer under approved permission):** “What did you make of the written feedback on that original submission? What did you agree with, question or choose not to act on, and why?” If the feedback is not available, discuss the participant's recollection without treating it as verified content.

**Transition:** “Tell me about the policy problem you're working on now. Which part has been hardest to think through? How does the way you work now compare with your formative assignment?”

**Reasoning:** “How did you decide what evidence mattered? What uncertainty or competing values made a choice difficult?” Follow up: “What led you to that decision? What might change your mind?”

**Feedback:** “Thinking of the written formative feedback or later peer feedback, which advice did you use, change or decide not to follow? Why?” Distinguish the original feedback itself from the participant's later account of acting on it.

**AI use:** “Have you used PolicyMemo.ai or another AI tool while working on this? What did you use it for? Tell me about a suggestion that helped, failed or made you reconsider something.” Follow up: “How did you decide whether to trust or question it?”

**Individual/group distinction:** “What came from your own thinking, what was shaped by peers/teaching, and what, if anything, came from AI? Are those contributions easy to distinguish?”

**Ownership of judgement:** “Which part of your current argument do you feel best able to defend? Where do you still feel unsure?” 

**Critical closing:** “Can you describe a case where AI made the work harder or less clear? What have I missed?”

**Optional authorised artefact prompt:** if the participant **separately consented** and the independent interviewer can lawfully access their shared work, use a selected, non-sensitive example to ask what the participant meant. Do not show their assessment marks or press for identifiable confidential details.

## Wave 2 — after grades, roughly six weeks after teaching where feasible (thirty to forty-five minutes, *excluding* task time if separate)

**Since the module:** “What kinds of policy, study or professional work have you done since IIPP0010? Have you approached any of these differently?”

**Continued AI practice:** “Do you use AI differently now? When do you deliberately avoid it? What do you ask it to do, and what do you want to decide yourself?”

**Judgement and learning:** “Is there something about how you frame problems, judge evidence or compare options that you now do differently? Can you recall a specific example?” Follow up: “Could that also have resulted from other teaching, experience or feedback?”

**Counterexamples:** “Have you found yourself agreeing with AI when you later thought it was wrong? Have you rejected useful advice? What happened?”

**Task discussion, if separately consented and undertaken:** “What was your first understanding of this unfamiliar problem? Why did you select those criteria and supporting evidence? What alternative would have changed your provisional judgement? What, if anything, would AI help with here?” This conversation does **not** substitute for an independent task.

**Closing:** “Has your confidence or independence changed? What do you think the module/tool helped with, did not help with, or made harder? What is a different explanation for that change?”

## Interviewer memo

After each interview (without showing the tutor prior to marking): record prompt variants used, notable example, contradiction/negative case, contextual limitations, follow-up questions, and observer inference **separately** from participant statements. Do not label accounts 'learnt' or 'dependent' without evidence.

## Analytical alignment

Use five policy domains: **problem definition, evidence, uncertainty, options/trade-offs, justification**. Connect interview accounts with eligible written work, observations and voluntary AI conversations only after consent and permitted linkage are established. Two interviews reveal *accounts of changing practice*, not causal AI effects.

# Structured seminar observation — draft protocol v0.1

**Status:** new research method requiring amendment. Original approved plan only permits **non-identifying, group-level teaching notes**, not consented individual observation records.

## Proposed setting

Two core PolicyMemo.ai sessions: Strategic Triangle and Thinking Hats, after their relevant teaching modifications have been approved for study purposes. The existing formative-feedback and other seminars can provide contextual non-AI teaching activity **without automatically becoming research observations**.

**Observer:** independent or trained member of research team [to confirm]. **No audio/video recording by default.** Distinguish observations permitted by consent from ordinary teaching and ensure safeguards for mixed-consent groups.

## Observation sheet

| Field | What to note |
|---|---|
| Session context | Date, activity, topic, group size, observer, which AI tool (if any), whether available to all, prompt/task conditions |
| Permitted unit of observation | Consenting participant/group only; method to prevent capturing a non-consenter's words or identity |
| Significant policy choice | A problem frame, causal claim, evidence interpretation, uncertainty, policy criterion, option or trade-off the students discuss |
| Contributions visible | A peer challenge, tutor prompt or AI critique **only when genuinely observed**; unknown contributions remain unknown |
| Reasons offered | Evidence, values, legitimacy, feasibility or uncertainty used to defend a position |
| Response | View retained, revised, rejected or deferred, with or without visible reasons |
| Notes about tool use | Whether AI critique was requested, how it was introduced, whether participants examined its reasoning |
| Context | Time pressure, task rules, prompts that specifically require justification, group interaction |
| Contradiction or alternative explanation | Did peer or tutor feedback already make the same point? Did the activity itself prompt the response? |
| Evidence confidence | Directly seen/heard; later reported; observer interpretation; insufficient evidence |

## Procedure

Before the session, identify the consenting population from the independent contact **without giving the tutor an identifiable participation list**. If mixed-consent groups cannot be observed without collecting non-consenters' contributions, **do not collect attributable research notes from those groups**. Agree a workable arrangement with UCL before claiming observations are feasible.

During teaching, observe without directing the students' reasoning; collect concise descriptive notes, not assumed thoughts. Afterward, separately note analytic hypotheses and negative cases. Obtain links to eligible worksheets or tool transcripts only by independently approved submission routes.

## Quality controls

Pilot the observation form with a **synthetic role-play**, then have the human researchers discuss ambiguous examples. Collect no identifying participant data on personal devices. Link to optional interviews only after approved consent/custody processes. The researcher-teacher must not access participation information before grades are final.

## Ethical and interpretive limits

Observation can illuminate how peer challenges, teaching prompts and AI are used, but **cannot read private cognition** or establish longer-term independent skill. Whole-room plenaries and contributions from non-consenting students should be recorded only as permitted anonymous/general teaching context, not attributable individual research data.

# Comparable individual policy task — design specification v0.1

**Status:** proposed new, ungraded research task requiring an ethics amendment, not an already-approved part of teaching. Intended as **optional**, separately consented follow-up after final grades.

## Why this is not simply a second interview

Interviews reveal how students explain and remember their learning. A separate task offers some **observable evidence of policy reasoning** in a new context. Neither alone establishes a causal impact of PolicyMemo.ai. The original course's final memo is an extended AI-eligible version of the *same* policy problem, so is not an independent transfer test.

## Proposed structure

- **Task A:** an **earlier individual** policy challenge genuinely comparable with Task B, completed as approved ordinary teaching or later as a prospective research task. Confirm what has actually occurred for the current cohort; **do not invent a prior baseline**.
- **Task B:** a **different unfamiliar scenario** after grades are final, of similar complexity, reading length, evidence quality, normative tensions, time allowance and instructions. Both should initially be completed **without AI and without peers**, with access conditions explicitly recorded.
- **Workload:** aim for around **15–20 minutes for each short written task** plus follow-up discussion; check participant burden in the approved information sheet. A short controlled artefact, not a full memo or graded exercise.
- **Scenarios:** choose two different public-policy areas to reduce simple topic familiarity, but ensure both require problem definition, evidence scrutiny, uncertainty, policy alternatives and public-value/feasibility trade-offs. Have an independent policy-teaching reviewer assess comparability before fielding.
- **Form:** a concise hypothetical briefing with two or three plausible but incomplete evidence statements, at least two possible options, affected groups and an institutional constraint. Avoid policy domains likely to solicit sensitive personal information. Provide all students the same available evidence per task; do not make the follow-up trivially easier.
- **Student response:** define the problem; explain which evidence is credible or missing; acknowledge one uncertainty; compare at least two options against defensible criteria; provide a provisional recommendation and explain what could change it.

**Optional second phase:** if ethically approved, after the individual unaided response, ask what role AI *might* play or let the student deliberately test an AI critique and revisit the position. **Do not combine the assisted phase score with the unaided task or label it an independent test.** If the tool is only discussed hypothetically, do not claim observed AI use.

## Analysis and comparability guardrails

Human researchers compare **the strength and appropriateness of reasoning**, not the number of changes, length of answers, 'AI-sounding' prose or correctness of one contested policy recommendation. Use the same broad prompts and documented domain-specific criteria across tasks. Where possible, reviewers analyse pseudonymised tasks without knowing temporal order, then review contextual traces. Have a second human reviewer critically examine borderline judgements and negative cases. Practice effects, maturation, other modules and selection into follow-up remain plausible explanations.

**What this may warrant:** examples of independently exercised policy judgement at two points (if truly comparable A/B), with cautious discussion of continuity or change. **What it cannot warrant alone:** that PolicyMemo.ai caused improvement or caused cognitive offloading.

## Practical stop/go decision

1. Is there an authentic **early comparable unaided task** already available with eligible consent? If not, consider a **future cohort** or limit current-cohort claims to post-module application.
2. Can the timing and new task be included in the amendment **before** data collection, and independent student contact maintained?
3. Is task burden proportionate for six to eight volunteers, and is a second coder feasible?
4. Does UCL approve the exact permitted support/recording conditions?

A field-ready scenario pack should be written and pilot-tested using **synthetic answers** only after these choices are made; this specification is enough to describe the research method at the first amendment-drafting stage.

# Data management and qualitative analysis — proposed amendment v0.2, human first

**Date:** 14 November 2026. **Status:** draft proposed processing plan, not a permission or security authorisation. The **original approved plan prohibits putting student research data into any AI analysis system**.

## A. What data is being proposed

| Data class | Collector / source | Research purpose | Currently permitted by original plan? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary seminar written work (opt-in) | Independent administrator / approved submission | Initial and later positions, rationale and revision | Yes, under original optional consent and handling conditions |
| Original submitted **formative policy problem definition and analysis** | Independent approved retrieval or voluntary submission; separate consent | Document early problem framing, evidence and causal reasoning, before PolicyMemo.ai-specific teaching | **Not explicitly authorised as a separate assessment record — amendment** |
| **Tutor's written formative feedback** | Independent approved retrieval or voluntary submission; **different consent** | Document an external critique and interpret reported feedback uptake | **Not explicitly authorised — amendment; verify staff feedback processing rights** |
| Voluntarily shared selected, redacted PolicyMemo.ai conversations | Student chooses what to share | Document what AI suggested and visible responses | Yes, under original optional consent |
| Final Personal Policy Problem memo | Approved consent; **access after marks final** | Continuity of policy argument | Yes, with separate consent and post-marking access |
| Non-identifying group-level teaching notes | Tutor as ordinary teaching context | Broad teaching process | Yes, not named individual observations |
| New structured consented classroom observation | Trained independent observer | Peer, teacher and tool contributions visible in seminar discussions | **No — amendment** |
| Two research interviews, potentially audio/transcripts | Independent interviewer during marking; later researcher only under revised safeguards | Students' accounts of decision-making and evolving practice | **No — amendment** |
| New optional later individual scenario task | Participant after grading | Independent application in unfamiliar problem | **No — amendment** |
| AI critique of participant analysis | UCL-authorised, specified service if approved | Check human themes, disagreements, alternative explanations | **No — separate amendment AND institutional data/security authorisation** |

## B. Human-led analysis protocol

1. Store all research originals, signed/online consent, recordings and identification key only in a specified **UCL-approved, access-controlled** location. Separate contact identity and material from the tutor/marker until grades final. Use unique participant codes, keep dates and permissions metadata, and maintain a data inventory with lawful retention/withdrawal limits.
2. Keep the **original formative script and written feedback as separate dated objects**. Compare the starting reasoning with what feedback actually recommended, and distinguish those recommendations from whether students said or showed they had acted on them. Document any reported prior use/non-use of general AI tools, but do not infer AI use from writing style.
3. Read each consented text and describe **what is visible**, distinguishing the student's words, researcher interpretation and unobserved group influences. Analyse the original five dimensions: problem framing, evidence, uncertainty, options/trade-offs and judgement/justification.
4. Identify significant policy decisions and any revisions, including **justified non-revision**. Compare tutor feedback, peer critique, agent suggestions and later work **only to the extent the sources permit**; don't infer who originated an idea from a polished final argument.
5. Develop and refine themes through **within-student** and **cross-student** comparison, with negative cases (defensible acceptance of AI, poor rejection of good advice, better work without a discernible rationale, reflection without change) and relevant contextual differences.
6. A second **human** researcher re-analyses a purposive sample and logs both agreements and disagreements; do not mislabel two LLM passes as independent human coders. Keep audit trail for changes to definitions and conclusions.
7. Distinguish assisted task quality from the exercise of judgement and from evidence of independent development. Do not claim causal effects from a small nonrandomised teaching study.

## C. Limited LLM critique: proposed workflow and decision gate

**Baseline mode (available without exposing participant material to AI):** humans prepare genuinely non-identifying **aggregate interpretations, hypothetical examples or literature-based arguments** in approved storage. A model can be asked to **challenge the logic** of these abstract summaries: “What rival explanations remain? Which claims go beyond the stated evidence? What negative cases should humans search for?” Researchers then check suggestions **against the controlled original participant data themselves**.

**Conditional extension:** using even name-redacted excerpts, interviews or work products in a model requires a clear and **specific** institutional decision covering (i) approval under the amended research protocol; (ii) information-security and data protection review; (iii) lawful processing basis and whether participant notice/permission is needed; (iv) whether the data are actually anonymous or pseudonymous; (v) AI provider/service identity, contractual processor terms, location and retention; (vi) training/secondary-use settings; (vii) permissions to reuse students' original work; and (viii) mitigations for re-identification of distinctive Personal Policy Problem topics. Do not assume a private GitHub repo or consumer subscription is a secure/authorised university analysis environment.

**If UCL does not approve it:** analyse the participant corpus **entirely by humans**. Keep literature review, synthetic examples and public aggregate methodology criticism separate.

## D. Record of model assistance, if and only if used lawfully

| Field | Example / definition |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Challenge the human's preliminary interpretation, not make primary coding or grade decisions |
| Service/model | Exact approved deployment and version |
| Inputs | Data classification, anonymisation and authorisation record; never copy participant data into this public/private documentation repo |
| Prompt | Research question posed to the model, saved in approved storage |
| Outputs | AI-suggested contrary reading, missing-case prompt, or logical flaw |
| Human review | Accepted / rejected / uncertain, and why; source verified against restricted originals |
| Contribution | Actual resulting analysis change, if any |
| Bias/limitations | Potential overgeneralisation, invented interpretations, sensitivity to prompt and confirmation bias |

**No claim that LLM critique improves quality without a bounded evaluation of the suggestions.**

## E. Ethics and data-protection checks before submission

- Confirm current approved PIS/consent documents, project identifier, actual UCL retention rules and secure recording/transcription tool.
- Confirm whether **legitimate institutional research processing** relies on consent as the legal basis or a separate lawful basis; participation consent and GDPR lawful basis are not necessarily the same legal concept. Do not amend this by assumption.
- Align withdrawal deadlines with both follow-up interviews and re-identification/key destruction.
- Avoid collecting third-party or workplace data; advise participants not to share them with PolicyMemo.ai.
- Ensure data cannot inadvertently be linked to academic marks or tutor/marker participation decisions.
- Obtain current UCL ethics and information-security/data-protection guidance for the **specific** tool and data classification; the original blanket prohibition remains in force until formally replaced.
