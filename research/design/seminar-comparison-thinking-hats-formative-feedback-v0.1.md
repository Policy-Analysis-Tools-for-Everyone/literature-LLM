## 1. Actual formative-feedback seminar

The first half (slides 3–13) asks students to reflect on their formative submission: what they were pleased with, what was challenging, one improvement, one strength to carry forward, whether analysis and evidence were strong enough, **why**, and questions about feedback. They then exchange policy problem statements and feedback in groups of three/four, examine an item of evidence and consider how to answer remaining questions.

The pivot on slide 15 explicitly links **interpreting, checking and selecting teacher feedback** to the demands of **evaluating AI-supported decisions**. The second half asks students about their prior AI use (including **not using AI**), concerns and limitations (slide 16), discusses dependency, accountability, epistemic coloniality, transparency and appropriate human intervention (slides 17–19), and then applies AI data-quality, delegation and authoriser-accountability questions to their own policy memo contexts (slide 20). Slide 21 asks what is missing from crowded policy discourse.

**This is a highly relevant teaching setting for policy judgement:** students use feedback on *their actual problem definition*, assess evidence and competing criteria, and think about when AI should/should not be delegated a role. **However, the deck does not itself specify a live PolicyMemo.ai interaction during the exercise**, nor a research observation protocol.

**Methodological strength:** teacher feedback already exists independently of the research proposal, and students are asked for individual reasoning before peer discussion. Potentially useful for investigating **how they understand and act on feedback and what counts as a defensible policy argument**.

**Methodological limits:** feedback may vary by marker/student; students have unequal experiences; self-reports of use are not tool logs; sharing formative drafts or identifiable tutor feedback may be an additional data class not authorised merely by observing teaching.

## 2. Actual Thinking Hats speed dating seminar

The Week 8 deck (slides 2–12) and linked document frame speed dating as **iterative peer learning and improvement of policy memos**. Students prepare a one-minute pitch of **problem, reader, alternatives, proposal and evidence**; pair up to present and receive feedback through a rotating hat; change the pitch; move partners; repeat for three rounds; and pitch to the whole room.

The perspectives in the source are:
- **White / Facts:** missing or unclear evidence; support for the problem/argument;
- **Black / Caution:** weaknesses, risks, objections and counterarguments;
- **Blue / Process:** coherence, logic and connection of problem, alternatives and proposal;
- **Red / Feelings and narrative:** audience response, persuasiveness and engagement;
- **Yellow / Optimism:** strengths and convincing features;
- **Green / Creativity:** new ideas, alternative approaches and presentation.

The supporting document says students **must note one piece of advice to incorporate** after a round. This is pedagogically understandable but **methodologically important**: it requires uptake and thus risks equating revision with good judgement.

**Methodological strength:** a repeated, observable sequence of human critique; different criteria for judging the *same evolving policy argument*; available snapshots of students' reasoning and what changed across rounds. Good fit for **evaluative judgement** and the Model C interest in situated practice.

**Methodological limits:** differing peer feedback, delivery skills and hat familiarity affect revision; the hats shape what criticisms are expressed; compulsory adoption confounds *good appraisal* with *following a procedural instruction*. A pitch's persuasive quality is not equivalent to its evidential quality. No direct AI use is required by the current deck, and peer-to-peer feedback is not automatically consented research observation.

## 3. How these sessions contribute differently to Model C

| Question | Formative feedback session | Thinking Hats session |
|---|---|---|
| **What prompts critique?** | Existing tutor feedback, the student's written policy problem and evidence | A peer using an explicitly assigned perspective on the student's memo pitch |
| **What could become visible?** | Interpretation and selection of feedback; confidence about strength of analysis; remaining research questions | Successive revisions, competing quality criteria, reasons for accepting, modifying or declining suggestions |
| **What about AI?** | Students **discuss** prior/prospective use and risks, including delegation and data quality | **No mandatory AI stage in the current deck**; a separate optional stage could be designed |
| **What needs caution?** | Reflection may be shaped by prior grading and social discussion; no continuous observation of cognition | Hats intentionally structure feedback; repeated practice can improve pitches without learning attributable to AI |
| **Distinct study value** | **How students evaluate feedback on their own work** | **How students respond to multiple perspectives while refining an argument** |

Together, they are stronger than an isolated 'AI did something' activity: they let the researcher investigate *human feedback literacy and situated critique* before introducing a specific AI comparison, without pretending feedback from different sources is interchangeable.

## 4. Proposed adaptations — preserve the teaching value

### A. Formative-feedback session — add one short optional research-friendly comparison (future approved iteration)

**Keep:** initial personal reflection; pair and group discussion; evidence-strength challenge; the existing bridge to AI-assisted decision-making.

**Candidate teaching artefact:** a brief **individual note at the beginning and end** asking:
- “Which part of your formative feedback matters most, and why?”
- “What would count as enough evidence to reconsider the way you've framed your problem?”
- “After discussing with peers, which feedback do you still think is useful, which is less useful, and why?”
- “Where, if anywhere, might AI be useful or unhelpful to the next stage of your analysis?”

**Reason:** compare *reasoned selection of feedback*, not simply satisfaction with marks. Observe both changes and justified decisions **not** to change. If used for research, opt-in permission and data handling need to be explicit; do not assume assessed scripts or tutor comments are anonymised research material.

### B. Thinking Hats — revise compulsory uptake to critical selection

**Keep:** six perspectives; one-minute pitch; rotating partners; successive rounds; final room presentation.

**Recommended wording change (proposed, not present in source):**
> “After each round, decide whether to **use, adapt or set aside** the advice you received. Make a brief note of **what the suggestion was**, **what you decided**, and **why**.”

This avoids treating obedience to a hat's suggestion as evidence of good judgement and permits **no change with reasons**. A student might correctly reject a confident but unsupported Black Hat risk claim or accept a mundane White Hat evidence correction.

**Minimal material for later comparison:** version 1 of pitch + 2–3 short decision notes + final pitch. Collect voluntarily under an approved protocol; do not record peer identities in the research artefacts by default. An observer needs separate permission to record statements by any named participant.

### C. Optional AI comparison — a new experimental/teaching proposal, not in either deck

After students have **already experienced human critique**, one possible later stage is to ask PolicyMemo.ai to evaluate the pitch against similar policy-specific questions, then have the student **assess the AI's advice**. Record the actual prompt/output **only if consented and approved** and preserve an equivalent non-AI peer-critique activity.

**Key confound:** if AI always comes last, apparent sophistication may reflect **three prior rounds of peer input, extra time and increasing familiarity**, not the AI's distinctive contribution. Do **not** describe the resulting comparison as causal. One could rotate the order in a future design if this question is central, but that increases intervention/ethics complexity and may be unnecessary for a small qualitative study.

**Better initial research purpose:** contrast *types of questions and reasons* students encounter through peers, formal teacher feedback and AI. Avoid simplistic ranking of which source is 'best'.

## 5. A coherent three-part research opportunity

1. **After formative feedback:** how do students interpret a teacher's critique, identify what matters and plan their next policy-analysis move? This makes the *assessment of quality* and *regulation of the work* visible.
2. **At the Thinking Hats seminar:** how do different peer perspectives influence the student's evolving policy argument and what is their own reasoning when adopting or rejecting advice? This brings *socially situated judgement* into view.
3. **At a distinct PolicyMemo.ai activity (if authorised):** how does the student interpret a tool's challenge or suggestion, and does it prompt different kinds of questioning or decision-making? This makes AI a distinct object of study instead of silently inserting it into a human-feedback exercise.

For the **later unfamiliar individual policy task**, match the substantive assessment demands (problem definition, evidence, uncertainty, alternatives, criteria and justification), but **do not simply re-run Thinking Hats**: this would not test independent judgement. An early, reasonably comparable individual task is essential if claiming temporal development.

## 6. Why these fit the literature without overclaiming

- **Tai et al. (2018) and Bearman et al. (2024)** support a conceptual focus on **evaluative judgement** about quality and AI-mediated processes, not that any particular hat or exercise causes learning.
- **Elkhodr & Gide (2026), audited C03:** structured AI-as-critic tasks can make accept/modify/reject reasoning visible. The activity's very structure may prompt that behaviour.
- **Farrokhnia et al. (2026), audited C04:** higher-rated AI feedback did not yield significantly better revisions; don't equate advice offered with learning.
- **Herdağdelen et al., audited C02:** upstream analytical decisions may be influenced even when final policy recommendation effects are not confirmed.
- **Yao (2025), Zou (2026), and the other postgraduate studies:** offer sensitising insights on regulation, not a basis to infer cognitive mechanisms from a pitch revision alone.

## 8. Suggested next design decision

**Recommendation:** include **both** seminars as complementary opportunities in the integrated design, but keep their educational purposes distinct. Use the formative-feedback session to study **how students assess and act on feedback**, and Thinking Hats to study **how students make decisions amid competing peer challenges**. Introduce any PolicyMemo.ai critique as a **clearly distinguished third activity**, rather than editing the source decks to imply that it was already part of them.

Once delivery dates and approvals are resolved, design **one minimal shared set of analysis prompts** so the same policy domains can be considered across both seminars without forcing all activities into one artificial experimental pipeline.
