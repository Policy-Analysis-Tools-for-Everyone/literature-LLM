# Methodology design discussion v0.3 — longitudinal interviews, later task, AI-supported analysis and theory options

**Prepared:** 14 November 2026. **Status:** proposals under discussion. **Researcher-confirmed:** Model C as the preferred framing; ability to redesign seminar teaching activities; interest in interviews during the module and around six weeks after its conclusion (after marking); interest in human-first, LLM-second analysis. **Not yet decided:** primary theoretical terminology, exact timings, interview arrangements, later task, AI processing authorisation, data collection instruments. No amended ethics approval is assumed.

**Source of original conditions:** `practice-as-software-research-plan.md`, supplied researcher-approved study plan. [Study baseline](../study/approved-baseline.md). ## 1. What stays central

**Preferred Model C question:** “When and how do students make, scrutinise and justify consequential policy-analysis choices in AI-supported practice, and what evidence—if any—suggests change in that capability over time?”

In participant-facing language: **How do students make decisions about policy problems while learning with AI, and does that experience influence how they approach later policy work?**

Do not replace the focal problem with AI essay quality or general trust in an LLM. Distinguish **(a) the quality of the answer; (b) judgement actually exercised and justified; (c) change over time**. The word “episode” is not needed in accessible materials; compare **significant decisions and revisions to policy reasoning**.

## 2. Proposed longitudinal data-collection sequence

**The user can design seminar activities around the research aims**, provided any research-specific additions are approved before collecting data. A structured seminar is a *learning intervention* as well as a potential observation setting; it may prompt the very explanations the researcher hopes to observe.

| Stage | Educational/research purpose | Candidate material | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Initial comparable individual seminar activity** | Document early framing, criteria, evidence and uncertainty without imposed AI critique | Short written policy analysis plus brief written grounds | Baseline need not be AI-naïve; any already completed early work cannot retroactively become a prospectively controlled baseline |
| **Structured seminar sequence during module** | See how original positions are examined by self, peers and PolicyMemo.ai, then returned to the individual | Individual → group → AI → individual products, changes and reasons; voluntary conversations | Requires separate consent for research use. Structured participant observation beyond generic non-identifying group notes requires ethics amendment |
| **First interview, during teaching** | Near-time account of tool practices, disagreements, how students choose support, perceived ownership and concerns | Approx. 20–30 min semi-structured interview, ideally anchored in the student's *own* authorised work | **Cannot be conducted by the teaching/marking researcher under original independence arrangement**. Candidate: independent interviewer, records segregated and inaccessible to lecturer until grades final; requires amendment |
| **Final course work** | Trace continuity in one policy problem and critically compare earlier reasoning | Final Personal Policy Problem memo, with optional consent, only after marks finalised | Same substantive topic and possible continued AI use mean this is not independent transfer |
| **Follow-up after final grades, roughly six weeks after module** | Examine evolving habits, recall, independent application and perceptions of AI's role beyond seminars | Second interview, approx. 30–45 min, plus short *new* but reasonably comparable individual policy scenario | **Interview alone is not a task**; consent and amendment needed; timing may affect original withdrawal deadline and project schedule |

**Sample planning:** a small purposively varied subset of perhaps **six to eight participants interviewed twice** = twelve to sixteen interviews. Attrition and sampling bias must be anticipated; this is qualitative understanding, not a powered effect-size test. If an independent interviewer cannot be secured for mid-course meetings, substitute an approved non-identifying teaching reflection or delay research interviewing until after marking, clearly noting the validity trade-off.

### What does 'new comparable individual follow-up task' mean?

It is a **separate short exercise embedded before or around the second interview**, not the interview itself. For example: a brief unfamiliar public-policy problem with limited conflicting evidence; ask the student to define the problem, identify what they do not know, select criteria, compare two plausible courses of action, and justify a provisional judgement. **First attempt without AI**, then optional discussion of whether and how AI assistance would change their method (or a deliberately separated AI-enabled second phase if approved).

To infer *change*, a **baseline task must be comparable** in complexity, prompt specificity, timing and allowed tools. A new task cannot make a causal AI-effect claim without a stronger comparison. Evaluate reasoning *qualitatively*, with attention to the substantive strength and context of explanations, not a mechanical score for disagreements or number of edits.

### Timing and consent risks

The originally approved plan keeps the teaching researcher unaware of participants until marking is final. Mid-course researcher interviews require **an independent interviewer or a substantially revised conflict-of-role arrangement**. The approved **31 March 2027 withdrawal deadline**, post-mark access to data, participant information, dates and retention terms must be reconciled with later interviews and any new task. Students should be told clearly that participation is voluntary and unrelated to marks.

## 3. What observations and interviews genuinely add

The original approved **written** materials show what students put on a page and (if shared) their AI prompts/responses, but not all group discussion, off-screen reasoning or why a change was made.

- **Structured observations** can illuminate the *sequence* of classroom deliberation, who contributes an alternative, how peers negotiate ideas, which tool practices are enacted, silences and how prompts/context affect behaviour. They remain selective, can alter classroom behaviour, and do not reveal internal mental states.
- **Near-time interviews** help interpret *why* students accepted, questioned or ignored advice; what they say they were uncertain about; what they do beyond PolicyMemo.ai; and whether the researcher is misreading a written change. Such reports may be rationalised.
- **Delayed interviews** ask how practice has changed after course demands disappear—continued AI use, professional/other studies, habits, reliance, changed confidence, and counterexamples. **Without a comparable task**, these remain self-reports, not an independent capability test.
- **The later individual task** can show application to a new problem under known support conditions; without a controlled comparison it should be called *suggestive evidence* of retained or transferable judgement rather than AI-caused development.

Triangulation means **actively checking for contradictions**, not making all evidence align.

## 4. Theory options explained before researcher selection

| Candidate | Plain-language meaning | What it would foreground | Weakness if alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Evaluative judgement** (Tai; Bearman) | Can the student decide **what good policy analysis looks like, and why**, including judging their own and AI's work? | Criteria, evidence relevance, policy trade-offs, reasons for acceptance/rejection, sound judgement of outputs and processes | May overfocus on assessment quality rather than politics, uncertainty, social processes and learning over time |
| **Metacognition / self-regulated learning** (postgraduate studies; Zimmerman theoretical lead) | Does the student **notice and manage their own thinking**—planning, monitoring, recognising uncertainty, changing approach? | How they decide to ask AI, cross-check it, seek perspectives and regulate effort | Self-description is not direct observation; frequent checking is not necessarily sound judgement |
| **Reflective practice** (Schön theoretical lead) | How do people **reconsider their framing and methods when real problems resist easy answers**? | Surprise, uncertainty, questioning assumptions, reframing, learning through doing | Hard to distinguish reflection-in-action from post-hoc explanations; original book chapters need full appraisal |
| **Practice perspective / strategy-as-practice** | How is policy work actually **done together**, through people, tools, routines and norms? | Roles of peers, teachers, model design, curriculum and sequences of action | Organisational strategy theory may not transfer neatly; does not itself establish good judgement or personal development |
| **Policy/professional judgement** (possible overarching domain framing) | Can a learner make **defensible public-policy choices** when evidence, public values, legitimacy, trade-offs and feasibility conflict? | Distinctive nature of public-policy reasoning, not just generic assignment quality | Needs clear links to established policy-analysis/public-value theory and transparent observable criteria |

**Current recommendation for deliberation, NOT an imposed decision:** keep *policy judgement* as the study's ordinary-language object; consider **evaluative judgement** as the most precise academic framework for **judging quality**, metacognitive/reflection literature as processes to investigate, and a practice perspective as a **corrective to individualistic explanation**. Avoid using four overlapping ‘validated scales’. Refer to [critical conceptual comparison](../../wiki/synthesis/conceptual-framework-v0.1.md); only Tai/Bearman original conceptual full texts have been source-reopened, while some other classical texts remain theoretical leads.

## 5. Human-first / LLM-second qualitative analysis — proposal, not permission

**Original approved plan expressly states:** “Nothing ... in any AI tool for analysis.” An amendment is necessary; redaction does **not** make a transcript anonymous automatically. Policy choices, unusual writing, voices, granular attributes and the existence of a separate linking key can render a participant identifiable.

**External guidance checked 14 November 2026:** [UCL information security guidance on third-party AI](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/information-security/information-security-policies-and-standards/guidance-use-third-party-ai-services-ucl) advises against inputting student/research/personal data; [UCL GenAI assessment guidance](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/teaching-learning/generative-ai-hub/three-categories-genai-use-assessment) specifically warns staff not to submit student work into AI tools; [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-sharing/anonymisation/pseudonymisation/) distinguishes pseudonymised from anonymous information. Research ethics approval alone does not overrule information-security and data-protection requirements. Confirm any specific approved UCL service and deployment with the responsible UCL teams **before** any upload.

**Proposed workflow with a tiered permission gate:**
1. **Human analysis first:** authorised researchers in UCL-controlled storage pseudonymise source materials, analyse them against a documented protocol, create candidate themes, retain contradictory examples and second-code a sample.
2. **Default AI use without actual student work:** provide only **synthetic examples, verified literature claims, or human-generated aggregate analytical propositions** that cannot identify participants; ask LLMs to challenge coding assumptions, point out gaps/alternative explanations, identify inconsistencies in theme definitions and propose negative-case checks.
3. **Conditional, only if authorisation explicitly covers it:** an appropriately approved UCL-controlled service might receive **genuinely anonymised/minimised extracts** for a specified purpose and retention period. Check re-identification risk, student IP, legal basis, consent information, processor terms, location, model-training settings, access and any DPIA. Redaction alone is not enough. Do not send pseudonymised transcripts to consumer tools under the current plan.
4. **Human verification and record:** every AI suggestion is rechecked against the untouched controlled data by the human researchers; record prompt, model/service version, allowable input class, AI proposal, human decision/reason, and any resulting analysis change. Do not treat AI as an independent human coder or a source of participant evidence.

**Study-design rationale to test:** the LLM is an **adversarial reviewer of a human interpretation**, not the primary analyst and not a judge of students' marks, intellectual capacity or character. A small within-research-team comparison of human-only versus human-plus-AI interpretations could help determine whether the AI actually improves detection of negative cases; it must not be treated as independent validation or automatically better quality.
