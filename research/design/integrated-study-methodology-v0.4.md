**Prepared:** 14 November 2026  
**Status:** substantive **proposal for researcher review**, to inform a future UCL IIPP LREC amendment. It **does not authorise** research interviews, structured observation, new research assessments, wider recruitment, or AI processing of participant data.

## 1. Research purpose and questions

The study investigates **how postgraduate students make, question and justify policy decisions when AI becomes part of their learning and analytical practice**. Its interest is not only the quality of a final policy memo, but also how students engage with uncertainty, consider others' reasoning, decide when to use AI, and explain their choices. A smaller longitudinal component explores whether these approaches appear in later work.

**Preferred working research question (Model C):**

> When and how do students make, scrutinise and justify consequential policy-analysis choices in AI-supported practice, and what evidence, if any, suggests change in that capability over time?

This is **proposed wording for a revised protocol**, not a replacement already approved by the ethics committee.

**Working subsidiary questions:**
1. How do students frame problems, assess evidence, work with uncertainty, select policy options and justify trade-offs during the module?
2. What happens to their reasoning through individual work, discussion with peers, seminar teaching and interaction with PolicyMemo.ai? What can be reliably attributed to each?
3. How and why do students accept, question, modify, reject or defer AI suggestions? How do they decide what constitutes a good or defensible policy argument?
4. How do students themselves describe changes in their AI-supported working practices, reliance, confidence and critical evaluation during the module and afterwards?
5. What do comparable individual tasks and later work suggest about the **exercise** of policy judgement in a different context, and where is there insufficient evidence to claim *development*?

## 2. Research approach

A **small longitudinal qualitative study embedded in authentic postgraduate policy teaching**. Use **purposefully designed teaching activities**, voluntary research contributions, selected classroom observations (if approved), student work, interviews and a later task. **Not** a randomised controlled trial, formal outcome evaluation of PolicyMemo.ai, or a claim of causal identification.

Proposed descriptive/interpretive analytic approach: **comparative qualitative analysis of students' policy reasoning over the teaching sequence and later accounts**, integrating within-student material when it is ethically and practically linkable. Keep an explicit distinction between a student, a peer group, a document version and a unit of talk/decision. Do not treat repeated observations from the same person as independent participants.

The original approved research plan limited recruitment to the researcher's **two seminar groups**. Wider sampling is a proposed amendment, not the current baseline. A possible **nested sample of approximately six to eight consented students** for longitudinal interviewing (two interviews each; twelve to sixteen interviews if all complete) is **a feasibility proposal**, not a statistically representative or powered sample. Sample across prior policy/AI experience, engagement and use patterns if the amendment and data-protection basis support collecting those descriptors. Do not require demographic collection just to create strata.

## 4. Proposed participant journey and distinct evidential purposes

| Point | What students do / source | What it helps examine | Research/ethics condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Beginning of module** | Existing individual problem statement / formative draft; where available, initial individual evidence/criteria task | Starting policy argument and way of justifying it | Only existing consented normal-course artefacts; cannot invent a prospective baseline retrospectively |
| **Seminars during teaching** | Individual response, peer deliberation, critique, reworked response; some sessions may involve PolicyMemo.ai | Visible changes and reasons within authentic policy-analysis work | Normal teaching may proceed; research-specific collection/structured observation needs approved amendment |
| **During-module interview** | Optional 20–30 min discussion soon after the relevant activity, referring to the student's own work and AI interactions | How and why students chose sources, questioned an assumption or used AI; other unrecorded influences; emerging tool habits | Proposed, not approved. Independent interviewer and inaccessible-to-teacher records until all grades are finalised |
| **Course submissions** | Formative problem definition; reflection essay; final memo and peer/oral defence | Continuity in one policy problem and students' explicit justification | **Final memo consented and accessed after marking** under approved plan; reflection essay/oral defence recordings are **not automatically approved research data** and would need explicit amendment permission |
| **After all grades released, ideally ~6 weeks after teaching ends if feasible** | Optional second 30–45 min interview **plus short new unfamiliar policy task** | Students' accounts of continued working habits and an opportunity to observe independently exercised reasoning on a different problem | Both proposed. Confirm grades, participant contact/withdrawal consent, scheduling and approved data collection |
| **Analysis and feedback** | Human cross-source interpretation, negative-case review and research reporting | What the assembled data warrant, conflicts and gaps | Human-only under original plan; conditional LLM-critical review requires separate approved data governance |

### Important contrast

The **mid-module interview** can illuminate how students *think they are changing while using the tool*. The **post-marking interview** can explore whether habits persisted when immediate assessment incentives fell away. The **new task** offers different evidence: how they approach an unfamiliar problem and explain choices under clearly described support conditions. **An interview alone is not a substitute for a comparable task.**

## 5. Proposed structured teaching activities

**Educational aim first.** These should be worthwhile seminars for **all** students regardless of research consent, with an equivalent non-AI route. They must not be designed to reward agreement with AI or with the researcher.

### Activity A — reasoned evidence and policy recommendation (adapt the existing evidence seminar)

Existing seminar source: social-media regulation simulation, uneven evidence, group critique, a one-page policy memo and defence. The original seminar already asks participants to state a position **before** reviewing evidence, and return to it afterwards.

**Refinement for a future approved research version:**
1. Individually, choose an initial provisional position and give **two reasons and one important uncertainty** (short, non-graded).
2. In small groups, appraise contrasting evidence and implications for different populations, choose whether to maintain/revise the proposed action, and note at least **one competing interpretation**.
3. The group receives **one short PolicyMemo.ai critique** of its own argument (where AI is used), **after** human work. Students decide which parts merit examination; an equivalent peer-critique route is available.
4. Each student individually returns to their recommendation, identifies the evidence/criteria that matter most, and explains either a change **or a defensible decision to stay with the initial view**.
5. Debrief the *nature of the reasoning*, not only who changed their view.

**Interpretive caution:** a required 'two reasons' prompt **produces written reasoning**, so such worksheets cannot be compared naively with unprompted ordinary work.

### Activity B — public value, legitimacy and operational capacity (adapt the existing Strategic Triangle seminar)

The supplied Seminar 5 plan is already structured as **individual answers → rotating peer groups → plenary**, separately for public value, legitimacy/support and operational capacity. It proposes a whole-room AI challenge at the end.

**Research-informed refinement for a future iteration:**
1. Each student identifies the public value sought, the constituency affected, a possible legitimacy challenge and the most binding operational constraint for their own personal policy problem.
2. Peers interrogate different parts of the triangle (and shift groups between sections, as the teaching plan already does).
3. In an AI-enabled condition, PolicyMemo.ai is used as a **critical reader** of the group's analysis rather than automatic author of a policy answer. Ask what assumptions are weak and what missing evidence would change the conclusion. Do not presume its critique is sound.
4. Each student records which part of their analysis still seems defensible, which question they need to investigate, and **how group and AI contributions differed** to the extent observable.
5. Discuss whether criteria conflict (for instance, strong expected public benefit but weak legitimacy or institutional capacity).

**Practical choice for researcher to decide:** a public-room demonstration of one volunteered policy problem is pedagogically valuable but gives a very different research trace from **private per-student AI work**. Do not treat these as equivalent interventions or confuse contributions from a demonstration with each individual's independent AI usage.

### Activity C — later individual policy problem (new, only if authorised)

A **short, genuinely unfamiliar but similar-complexity** policy scenario, with bounded conflicting evidence and contestable options. First complete a short individual **no-AI** response covering problem, relevant evidence, uncertainties, criteria, provisional course of action and reasons. Then, if the design requires it and is approved, have a **separate optional AI-enabled reflection stage** or ask what they would do with AI. The latter is a self-report, **not observed actual use**.

**Comparison:** only a **similarly structured earlier individual task** gives grounds to discuss development under comparable support conditions. Do not compare a highly scaffolded post-course task with an unstructured first seminar post-it and call the difference independent learning.

## 6. Interview guides (working sketches)

### Interview 1 — during teaching (20–30 minutes)

Start from a **specific piece of voluntarily shared student work**, where access and independence safeguards permit.

- Tell me about a policy decision in your current problem that was difficult. What made it difficult?
- How did you decide which evidence, values or practical constraints deserved weight?
- What were you thinking before speaking to peers or using AI? What changed, if anything?
- Can you describe a suggestion from PolicyMemo.ai that you accepted, questioned or rejected? **What was your reasoning?**
- Did the tool make you consider something you had missed, or make a persuasive argument you later found weak?
- How do you decide **when to use AI and when to work without it**?
- What part of the final analysis do you feel you can independently explain or defend?
- What else influenced this decision (teaching, reading, classmates, professional experience)? What have we not asked?

### Interview 2 — after marking, with task (30–45 minutes plus task time)

- Since the module, have you used AI for other research, study or professional tasks? When and why?
- Have your criteria for trusting or testing an AI argument changed? An example would help.
- What, if anything, have you carried forward from the seminars when approaching a new policy problem?
- What seems easier, harder or more dependent on external assistance now?
- Have you had an instance where working without AI produced a better result—or where AI improved your judgement? Can you describe it?
- **After the unfamiliar task:** how did you define the problem; why those criteria and evidence; what alternative would have changed your view?
- If you wanted AI assistance with this task, what would you ask it to do, and what would you not delegate?
- Looking back, did anything about the module or tool produce less independent thought? What counterexample best challenges your answer?

**Interview protection:** if held before marking, an **independent interviewer** must manage contact and recordings/transcripts. The teacher-researcher should not access identifying data or participant responses until grades are final, consistent with whichever amended ethics arrangements are actually approved.

## 7. Structured observation protocol — proposed only

For consented sessions under an approved amendment, an independent observer records **what can actually be seen/heard**, not supposed internal thinking.

**Header:** session/date/activity; whether AI was available and how; observer role; group size; extent of consent and how non-consenting students are protected; notable task/prompt differences.

**Descriptive fields:** question or dilemma at issue; who visibly introduced an option (only if consent permits recording that); evidence/criteria invoked; peer challenge; tool response; explicit uncertainty; subsequent decision or deferral; contextual factors (tutor prompting, time limits, assignment pressures); material linked to notes.

**Interpretive fields (kept separately):** candidate significance; rival explanation; researcher confidence; data gap. Do not quote or characterise a non-consenting student. Whole-class non-identifying teaching notes from the original protocol are not equivalent to new individual research observation.

## 8. Human-led qualitative analysis

Maintain five **policy-specific domains** from the approved plan: **problem definition; evidence; uncertainty; options and trade-offs; justification/judgement**. Use these as sensitising categories, then permit inductive themes from the material.

**Suggested analytical stages:**
1. **Prepare source inventory and cases** in approved UCL storage, with metadata for timing, task, AI availability, form of consent and who has access. Maintain the independent key with the approved custodians.
2. **Human-first reading and descriptive comparison** of earlier work, later seminar work and available tool conversations, separately documenting what was said/changed and what is inferred.
3. **Identify consequential decisions and revisions** in ordinary prose: what position the student initially held, which challenges are documented, what they subsequently did, and the reasons that can actually be evidenced.
4. **Compare across settings and time**, paying attention to different AI use patterns, individual versus group contribution, unprompted versus prompted justifications, and cases that do not follow the preferred account.
5. **Second human coder** independently reviews a purposive subsample; log disagreements, resolve categories and retain differences in interpretation where appropriate. Do not manufacture inter-rater agreement or reduce interpretive quality to a single statistic.
6. **Only under appropriate institutional permission**, ask an LLM to critique **non-identifying human-written thematic summaries or other specifically authorised inputs**: what claims are weak, what competing explanation or negative case should be checked, and where interpretations exceed observations. Researchers return to the original protected materials and decide whether to alter the analysis. Log model, prompts, disclosed inputs, advice, human action and rationale.
7. Report **qualitative patterns, divergent cases and participant counts** rather than spurious percentages/effect sizes, consistent with the original plan. Keep counterexamples visible.

**Important:** redacted or pseudonymised quotes are not automatically anonymous. The original ethics approval explicitly prohibits AI analysis of student material. **Actual participant work, transcripts and observations must not be supplied to an AI tool** unless an amended ethics and UCL information-security/data-protection authorisation specifically permits the relevant service, retention, terms and data type. Human-only analysis remains fully viable.

## 9. Quality and rival-explanation checks

- **Evidence of development:** later independent performance is a stronger claim than improvement in the same assisted task; an AI-supported final memo is not an unaided transfer test. [Wiles C01](../methods/ten-claim-evidence-audit-2026-11-09.md).
- **Source versus uptake:** more sophisticated AI critique does not entail better student reasoning. [Farrokhnia C04](../methods/ten-claim-evidence-audit-2026-11-09.md).
- **Prompted explanations:** AI-as-critic formats may elicit explanations precisely because the activity demands them; not proof of unprompted individual competence. [Elkhodr & Gide C03](../methods/ten-claim-evidence-audit-2026-11-09.md).
- **Upstream influence:** what changed in framing or criteria matters, not just the final recommendation; do not overstate final causal effect. [Herdağdelen C02](../methods/ten-claim-evidence-audit-2026-11-09.md).
- **Self-selection:** volunteers willing to share chat logs or re-attend a later interview may differ from others; report attrition and non-response.
- **Conflicts of role:** teaching, grading, participant identification, product development and analysis by the researcher are separated where feasible; avoid deciding the product helped students merely because it is the intervention being studied.
- **Teaching as a cause:** the module deliberately teaches methods of reasoning. Changes may reflect teaching, peer challenge, repeated tasks and feedback, not AI alone.
- **AI differences:** PolicyMemo.ai operates through external model platforms; record which service/version was used if consented and approved, without constructing an uncontrolled model comparison.

## 10. Ethics and feasibility changes required later (NOT approved)

The original protocol permits optional normal-course work, selected voluntarily shared tool conversations, final memos after marking and non-identifying teaching notes. It explicitly **excludes interviews, questionnaires, audio/video recordings, named individual observation and AI analysis of research data**.

A new application or amendment would need:
- updated research question, longitudinal design, participant contacts and recruitment beyond two groups if desired;
- how consent, withdrawal, teaching/marking independence and non-participation are protected;
- two interview waves, independent mid-course interview handling, any audio recording/transcription, and realistic participant burden;
- new unfamiliar policy task and how earlier tasks are meaningfully comparable;
- structured classroom observation, recording only consenting individuals and managing mixed-consent groups;
- any collection of prior AI use, confidence, demographic or platform data;
- clear data locations, identification key, de-identification thresholds, retention and **AI processing decision** separate from simple redaction;
- actual module timing, grading release, post-marking follow-up and withdrawal deadline (the original 31 March 2027 date must be checked);
- any changes to existing participant information and consent documents, ethics approval and information-security review.

**No participant research material is stored in this repo.**

## 11. Decisions to make before drafting the ethics amendment

1. **Recruitment:** current two groups or all four, and how independent consent and interviewer staffing will work.
2. **In-module cohort reality:** what was already completed by 14 November 2026, what approved research data exist, and which activities can actually be conducted prospectively this term.
3. **Interview design:** number of participants, within-module access independent of marker, and post-mark follow-up feasibility/attrition.
4. **Comparability:** choose earlier and later *individual* policy tasks with similar evidence, guidance and support conditions; if not feasible, moderate claims about development.
5. **The role of AI:** shared demonstration, individual feedback, model-choice/context, and optional non-AI comparator.
6. **Research data governance:** agree human-only default and seek approval for tightly scoped LLM critical review, without assuming redaction solves identifiability.
