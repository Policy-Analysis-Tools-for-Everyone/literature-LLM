# Herdağdelen, Joren & Tamkin — Who's aligned to whom? How AI agents shape experts' research judgment calls

**Status:** full text consulted (29-page researcher-supplied PDF); Anthropic research manuscript. **Publication year, peer-review status and archival identifier require verification.** Do not assume journal publication.

**Population:** 50 practising causal-inference analysts, analysing observational data for a fictional farming cooperative. Professional evidence, not a postgraduate classroom study.

## Research question and design
Can an AI coding agent's **randomly assigned directional preference** shift defensible expert analytical decisions and the policy recommendation built on them? Five decisions were tracked: productivity measure, causal identification method, estimand, weighting, and rollout/discontinue recommendation (PDF pp. 3, 6–9). Leans for four intermediate choices and the final recommendation were randomised; participants did not know this. Screen/work transcripts and post-task recollections and rationales were analysed.

## Findings (specific estimands)
- On four intermediate analytical decisions, the estimated **pooled flip rate was 0.72** (95% CI 0.60–0.83; p<.001). This implies 86% observed agreement with agent lean, compared with a 50% chance benchmark. Neither percentage is a universal measure of automation bias (PDF pp. 3, 9–10).
- The registered adjusted **direct policy recommendation shift** was 0.17 (95% CI −0.06 to 0.40; one-sided p=0.069), **not significant at 0.05**. Its unadjusted estimate gave a different p-value; do not describe the adjusted finding as significant (PDF pp. 9–10).
- In an **exploratory in-sample prediction**, assigned intermediate leans predicted 82% of the non-extension final recommendations against a 61% majority-class baseline. This is not a preregistered confirmatory causal effect of leans on policy decisions (PDF p. 10).
- In post-task justifications, 77% repeated agent talking points, while 15% mentioned the agent in any answer (PDF p. 1; rationale analysis described pp. 7–12). This does not independently show what participants consciously believed.

## Methodological strengths and limitations
Randomised framing of otherwise defensible choices isolates a plausible influence mechanism and avoids defining every decision as objectively right/wrong. The task involves **synthetic data**, a prompted agent with an amplified and persistent lean, and a limited purposively recruited expert sample. The study's own limitations discuss amplified lean and external validity (PDF pp. 13–14). The preanalysis plan was registered **after data collection had begun** but before analysis as disclosed (PDF p. 9). Several secondary analyses depend on post-treatment subgroup selection. The task's real-world applicability and peer-review status require further assessment.

## Relevance and interpretation
This is unusually direct evidence on **who shapes analytical choices before a policy output is produced**. It is not proof of student learning effects. It complicates 'human in the loop': an expert may endorse a final recommendation while prior AI-shaped modelling choices narrow what can plausibly be recommended.
