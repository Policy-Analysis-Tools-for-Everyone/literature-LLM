# Fernandes et al. (2026) — AI makes you smarter but none the wiser

**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108779  
**Publication:** *Computers in Human Behavior* 175, 108779 (2026); online October 2025.  
**Source:** researcher-supplied 18-page full PDF, uploaded with a misleading Chen-style filename; identity verified by title and DOI. **Role:** priority contextual *adult* experimental evidence, not postgraduate-specific. **Status:** initial full-text appraisal.

## Question and design
The authors examine **actual AI-assisted logical-reasoning performance versus participants' estimation of their own performance**. Study 1 analysed 246 Prolific adults who solved twenty LSAT-style reasoning items using ChatGPT and compared performance with a historical no-AI dataset (3,543 people). The authors explicitly describe this comparison as **quasi-experimental**, not causal (PDF pp. 4–6). Study 2 analysed 452 participants (245 AI; 207 no-AI) in a new AI/no-AI comparison, with an incentive to estimate performance accurately (PDF pp. 11–12).

## Observed results
In Study 1, the AI-assisted group averaged **12.98/20 correct** compared with **9.45/20** in historical controls, but estimated approximately **16.50/20** correct (PDF pp. 6–7). Study 2 similarly found higher AI-assisted scores (**13.31 versus 9.71**) and overestimation in *both* groups (PDF p. 11). Notably, the **AI and no-AI groups had similar trial-level confidence-discrimination AUC** in Study 2 (**0.62 vs 0.61**); so it is inaccurate to infer that AI *caused a drop in sensitivity* on the basis of those AUCs (PDF p. 12). The authors' model suggests a flattened Dunning–Kruger relationship with AI, but this is a model-dependent interpretation.

## Appraisal and limits
Strengths: objective and self-estimated scores, trial-level confidence ratings, two large samples, and a new no-AI group in Study 2. Important limitations: historical-group comparisons in Study 1; LSAT-style multiple-choice items rather than ill-structured professional policy tasks; no independent longitudinal learning outcome; mixed adult educational backgrounds; AI use required for every item. The paper's own discussion notes these limits (PDF pp. 14–15). Self-rated AI literacy is not objectively demonstrated competence. Avoid interpreting the result as AI inevitably eroding judgement.

## Relevance
Shows why **self-perceived understanding, demonstrated performance and reasoning provenance must be distinguished**, but should not draw us into generic AI credibility/error-checking research.
