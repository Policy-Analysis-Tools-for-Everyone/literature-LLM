# **Practice as Software: AI, learning and the exercise of policy judgement**

*Preliminary literature review draft v0.1 — 14 November 2026. Working prose for discussion with David Eaves; not a completed review.*

Something interesting is happening in the research on generative AI and learning. We are getting better at understanding what people can produce with AI, but we know considerably less about what happens to their thinking while they produce it.

This is important. If a student produces a stronger policy memo with the help of an AI system, what have they actually demonstrated? They may have developed a better understanding of their policy problem. They may have learnt to evaluate evidence more carefully or become better at weighing competing options. Equally, they may have produced a more convincing piece of work because the system did some of that thinking for them. Looking at the finished memo alone makes it difficult to distinguish between these possibilities.

The emerging literature provides good reasons to take this question seriously, although the evidence is more complicated than the familiar arguments about AI either enhancing or diminishing human intelligence.

Wiles et al. (2026), for example, found that professional consultants using AI performed better on technical tasks, but did not demonstrate a statistically significant advantage on a short subsequent assessment without AI. This doesn't establish that participants failed to learn. The assessment was narrow and immediate. It does, however, illustrate an important distinction between improving someone's performance on a task and developing the capabilities they might carry into another.

Other studies complicate the picture further. Farrokhnia et al. (2026) found that more highly rated AI feedback did not necessarily translate into greater improvements in students' revised essays. Fernandes et al. (2026) found that AI improved performance on logical-reasoning questions, while participants in both AI and non-AI conditions struggled to accurately assess how well they had performed. Taken together, these studies suggest that the quality of the work, the quality of the assistance and the quality of the person's own judgement are related, but different, things.

This study is interested in what happens between receiving assistance and producing an answer.

Consider a student developing a policy recommendation. They might begin by defining a problem, identifying its underlying causes and deciding which evidence matters. An AI system might then suggest a different interpretation, introduce an alternative explanation or challenge an assumption. The student now has a decision to make. Is the suggestion reasonable? Does it change their understanding of the problem? What evidence would help them decide? Should they accept it, adapt it or reject it?

These are not simply decisions about whether the AI is right or wrong. They involve judgements about the problem itself, the quality of the evidence and the consequences of choosing one interpretation over another.

Research on evaluative judgement offers a useful way into this question. Tai et al. (2018) describe evaluative judgement as the capability to make decisions about the quality of one's own work and the work of others. Bearman et al. (2024) extend this thinking to generative AI, suggesting that learners need to evaluate not only AI-generated outputs, but the processes through which those outputs are produced. This shifts our attention from the finished answer towards how people decide what constitutes good work in the first place.

Yet policy analysis presents a particular challenge. A good policy recommendation isn't simply a technically correct answer. Policy problems are often contested, evidence is incomplete, and competing options involve choices about values, legitimacy, feasibility and risk. A student may have to decide not just whether an AI-generated argument is accurate, but whether it provides a defensible account of a public problem and what ought to be done about it.

There is also a social dimension to this process. Students don't develop their arguments in isolation. Their thinking is shaped by conversations with peers, feedback from educators, the policy frameworks they are taught and increasingly the AI systems they work with. A recommendation that appears to represent an individual's judgement may have emerged through a much more complicated sequence of questions, challenges, revisions and decisions.

This is where we think the research needs to go further. **Rather than asking only whether AI improves the quality of students' work, we want to understand how students exercise judgement while working with it.** When do they question an argument? How do they decide which suggestions are worth pursuing? What happens when a peer, educator or AI system challenges a position they've already taken? And what, if anything, can these interactions tell us about the development of independent policy judgement over time?

The distinction is important because accepting an AI suggestion is not necessarily evidence of dependency, just as rejecting one is not necessarily evidence of critical thinking. Both can be thoughtful decisions, and both can be poorly reasoned. What matters is how the student arrives at that decision and whether they can explain and defend it.

Our interest, then, is not simply in what AI enables students to produce, but in **how policy judgement is exercised, challenged and potentially developed through working with AI and other people.**
