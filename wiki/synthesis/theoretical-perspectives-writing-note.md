# Explaining policy judgement and the complementary theories — writing note

**Status:** retained for the critical narrative literature review and eventual research paper; **researcher-endorsed framing (14 November 2026)**, but **not yet final scholarly prose**. The researcher selected **Model C: capability-in-practice**, agreed that *policy judgement* should be the substantive focus, and endorsed the complementary use of the concepts below. The precise weighting of frameworks can still change after full theoretical reading.

**Use when writing:** the review's theoretical framing; the study rationale; later discussion of interpretations and methodological limitations. This note is deliberately written for readers rather than as a coding taxonomy.

## What do we mean by policy judgement?

Policy analysis involves more than producing a technically convincing memo. Students must define uncertain public problems, judge competing evidence, consider who is affected, assess what public value means, compare feasible options and justify choices where reasonable people can disagree. **Policy judgement** is therefore the substantive subject of this study: how students make and defend decisions when evidence, values and practical constraints interact.

This emphasis is grounded in IIPP0010's own teaching aims, the approved research plan's five dimensions of analysis, and policy theory in the module syllabus. It is **our proposed study framing**, not a claim that the AI-education literature has established one universally accepted definition of policy judgement.

## Evaluative judgement: assessing what good work looks like

Evaluative judgement is the capacity to decide what constitutes quality in one's own work and the work of others. Tai et al. (2018) stress that learners must do more than recognise standards: they need opportunities to exercise judgements about quality in context. Bearman et al. (2024) extend the discussion to the generative-AI setting, including assessment of AI-generated outputs, assessment of AI-mediated processes, and AI assessment of the student's own judgements.

In our study, this matters when a student decides whether their problem definition is adequate, whether evidence supports a causal claim, whether competing policy options have been fairly compared, or whether an AI recommendation is appropriate. **Accepting AI advice can demonstrate good evaluative judgement if the grounds are defensible; disagreeing with AI does not automatically demonstrate it.**

**What it explains:** the *quality and grounds* of choices.  
**What it may miss:** politics, group practice, the development of thought over time and broader public value. It is a conceptual framing, **not direct empirical proof that using PolicyMemo.ai improves learning**.

## Metacognition and self-regulated learning: monitoring our own thinking

Metacognition refers to how people recognise, monitor and manage their own cognitive activity. Self-regulated learning concerns how learners plan an approach, monitor progress and adapt their strategies. A postgraduate student may recognise that they need better evidence, test an assumption, ask AI for a counterargument or decide to stop using AI and reconsider the problem themselves.

Qualitative postgraduate studies by Yao et al. (2025) and Dai (2026) describe such planning, monitoring and evaluation during AI-supported writing and reading. Zou et al. (2026) reports no significant overall difference in metacognitive experience between students choosing AI and those not choosing it, although some task-specific strategies differ. These papers **describe different outcomes and do not establish that using AI causes more or less metacognition**.

**What it explains:** how students *manage* their reasoning, including choices about using or questioning a tool.  
**What it may miss:** someone can monitor their thinking carefully and still make a weak or unjust policy judgement; visible written explanations are not direct access to cognition.

## Reflective practice: reconsidering the problem while doing the work

Schön's reflective-practice tradition concerns how practitioners respond to uncertain and ambiguous situations, including questioning how a problem has been framed rather than simply applying a standard solution. In a policy seminar, a student might discover that an apparently straightforward problem of public awareness involves deeper institutional barriers, leading them to reconsider the question they should be answering.

**What it explains:** learning through challenging assumptions, responding to surprise and reframing difficult problems.  
**What it may miss:** reflection during action is not directly visible in a revised document, and retrospective interview accounts may be reconstructions. **Schön's primary texts are still on our targeted full-text reading list**; this paragraph is an interpretive starting point, not a completed original-text appraisal.

## A practice perspective: reasoning happens with people, tools and institutions

Practice-oriented perspectives ask how work is actually accomplished through conversations, routines, methods, norms, artefacts and tools. Strategy-as-practice offers one established vocabulary for examining practitioners, practices and the flow of action (Jarzabkowski, Balogun and Seidl, 2007). In the classroom, a student's view may be challenged by peers, shaped by a policy framework, tested by PolicyMemo.ai, and then revised in their own writing.

**What it explains:** why reasoning cannot always be attributed to an isolated student or a single AI interaction.  
**What it may miss:** describing collective activity is not the same as demonstrating good judgement or a change in individual ability. Strategy-as-practice emerged from organisational strategy; its applicability to policy education needs explicit justification, and the original text has not yet received a complete source appraisal.

## How they fit together in Model C

These theories need not compete for the title of ‘the one framework’:

- **Policy judgement** names **what** we want to understand in the substantive policy setting.
- **Evaluative judgement** sharpens our account of **how the quality of policy analysis is assessed and justified**.
- **Metacognition and reflective practice** offer different perspectives on **how students monitor, question and reconsider their reasoning**.
- **Practice perspectives** help explain **the conditions in which that reasoning occurs**, particularly the contributions of peers, teachers, curricula, methods and AI.

This is the **agreed working conceptual orientation**, not a claim that the four traditions form one empirically established causal model. The study should test whether each adds explanatory value and retain observations that run against the preferred account.

## Literature-review-ready bridge paragraph (draft; refine after primary reading)

> Understanding the implications of generative AI for policy education requires distinguishing the quality of students' work from the judgement exercised in producing it. Evaluative judgement provides a way to examine how learners determine the quality of their own and others' work, including AI-generated material and AI-mediated processes (Tai et al., 2018; Bearman et al., 2024). Research on metacognitive regulation directs attention to the strategies learners use to plan, monitor and reconsider their work, although reported strategies must not be mistaken for independently demonstrated capability (Yao et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2026). Reflective practice provides a further lens on problem framing and learning in uncertain situations, while a practice perspective draws attention to how reasoning is shaped through interactions among students, educators, methods and tools. Taken together, these are complementary perspectives for investigating policy judgement as it is exercised in educational practice; they do not establish that AI use causes its development. The study therefore examines how students make and justify significant analytical choices, and considers separately what evidence indicates change in their capability over time.

**Editorial caution:** add checked original Schön and relevant practice-theory citations before presenting the last two sentences as a fully supported theoretical argument. Policy-specific literature from Bardach, Moore and the IIPP0010 readings is also needed to ground the *substantive content* of judgement.
